## 3. Structure: fund-letter, not essay

Sri's recurring correction to drafts: he writes as an **analyst-operator, not an essayist**. The structure that fits him:

- **Short paragraphs.** Two to four lines each. White space is structural on LinkedIn.
- **Single-line beats** as pivots between movements. A line on its own that turns the argument.
- **Named subpoints on analytical posts.** When a thesis has parts, name them as discrete points rather than dissolving them into flowing prose. This is the fund-letter feel. (But do not turn every post into a listicle; named subpoints suit thesis pieces, not short explainers.)
- **The thesis usually arrives early**, then the post earns it. Sri does not bury the lede under setup.

Avoid: long unbroken essay paragraphs, ornamental transitions, a wind-up before the point.

## 4. Opening hooks

The first line must do real work and pull in a reader who does not care about crypto. Patterns that fit Sri:

- **A market observation.** "Most of India's best companies still cannot raise money from most of India." (familiar, slightly uncomfortable, true)
- **A familiar-finance fact stated plainly.** "A wire transfer between two banks still takes two days to settle. In 2026."
- **A reframe-in-waiting.** A sentence that sets up the "that is not X, it is Y" move two beats later.
- **A human or market moment** that is Sri's own observation, not a reported anecdote. "I spent a decade moving money through the Indian banking system as a founder. The friction was never the technology."
- **A number with weight.** A specific figure the reader can feel.

Avoid: "In today's fast-paced world", "We are living in a time when", any throat-clear. Avoid opening with a crypto term the reader doesn't know.

## 5. The reframe move (Sri's signature)

Sri's single most characteristic device, surfaced directly from his own editing:

> "That is not a technology gap alone. It is a market access gap."

The pattern: **name what something is *not*, then name what it *is*.** It lands the thesis by correcting a too-easy reading. Use it **once per post, at the thesis moment**. It is powerful precisely because it is rare.

**Critical:** this is also an AI tell when overused or when the contrast is hollow. Three "not X, it's Y" constructions in a post is an instant fail. One, earning the thesis, on real contrast, is Sri. The difference is whether the *not* clause is a real misreading worth correcting, or just rhetorical scaffolding. Hollow: "It's not about money, it's about trust." (says nothing) Real: "That is not a technology gap. It is a market access gap." (corrects the diagnosis).

## 6. Hedged modals and the open ending

**Hedged modals.** On claims about contested or future outcomes, Sri uses "can", "could", "can make", "can bring" rather than flat prediction. "Tokenization can bring these assets within reach of a far larger base of investors" — not "Tokenization will democratise finance." The hedge is not weakness; it is an operator's honesty about what is not yet settled.

**The open ending.** Sri ends on **unresolved tension or a quiet observation**, never a tidy verdict, never a punchline, never "What do you think?". The reader should leave with something to wrestle with. From his real calibration: he chose to end a post on "genuine uncertainty rather than a declared outcome." The close is a question the reader now carries, not an answer handed to them.

Avoid closes like: "The future is on-chain." / "And that is why DeFi wins." / "The revolution has begun." / any line that resolves the tension the post opened.

## 7. The finance → DeFi bridge

This is the engine of every post. The structure:

1. **Familiar ground.** Open in mainstream finance the Indian non-crypto reader already knows — settlement delays, fund minimums, who can access which assets, fees, counterparty risk in plain terms, the gap between who has capital and who can deploy it.
2. **Name the friction precisely.** What specifically is wrong, and *why* it is structural rather than incidental. This is where the operator's eye shows.
3. **The reframe.** Show that the friction is not a bug to be patched but a property of how the system is built — and that an on-chain / tokenized / decentralised structure removes the cause, not the symptom.
4. **The honest edge.** What is still unsettled (custody, regulation, liquidity). Sri does not pretend DeFi has won; he shows where it is structurally better and where the open questions are.
5. **Open ending.** Leave the reframe sitting with the reader.

The thought-leadership is in steps 2 and 3 — the precision of the diagnosis and the structural reframe — not in crypto vocabulary.

## 8. Banned phrases and AI-tell library

Rewrite or cut on sight. These are the patterns that make a draft read as AI or as not-Sri.

**Mirror / parallelism tells**
- Sentence-fragment triads: "Concrete. Specific. Named." → use commas or a full sentence.
- Rhetorical triplets / tricolons used for cadence ("faster, cheaper, fairer"). Sri's LinkedIn voice does not lean on these the way some voices do. Cut.
- Mirror constructions ("X is Y. Z is W.") except a single one at the genuine thesis moment.
- "not X but Y" / "not X, it's Y" more than once (see §5).

**Performative-depth tells**
- "quietly" applied to public events ("the market quietly shifted").
- Standalone one-word dramatic paragraph ("Money." on its own line).
- "Here's the thing", "The truth is", "Make no mistake", "Let that sink in".
- "the part that matters most", "now, the part that matters", and similar setup phrases that announce significance instead of demonstrating it.
- Trailing flourishes: "And that is the story." / "That is the question." / "Welcome to the future."

**Punctuation / format tells**
- Em dashes anywhere. Use commas, colons, or semicolons. (En dashes only for number/date ranges.)
- Hashtags. Emojis. Bold-everything stacks.
- "What do you think?" / "Thoughts?" closes.

**Diction tells**
- "game-changer", "groundbreaking", "revolutionary", "paradigm shift".
- "slowly and then suddenly" (overused crypto cliché).
- "algorithm" used loosely for AI (too reductive).
- generic intensifiers: "incredibly", "massively", "hugely".
- tribal chain positioning (cheerleading one chain over another).

**Structural tells**
- Tidy-bow close that resolves the tension (see §6).
- A draft that feels too clean, too elegantly resolved. Sri's posts have an edge — a line that sits slightly underbaked, a close that compresses without being clever. If it sounds like content, it is too AI.

## 9. Reporter-tone library (the thing to strip)

Akshay's explicit instruction: thought-leader tone, NOT reporter tone. Strip every framing that narrates rather than asserts. Sri's own perspective leads; sources corroborate — never the reverse.

Cut on sight:
- "His story stopped me cold." / "stopped me in my tracks." / "I couldn't stop thinking about it."
- "In a recent conversation," / "I recently spoke with," / "Last week I sat down with,"
- "What struck me was" / "What caught my attention" / "I was fascinated to learn"
- "He told me" / "She explained that" / "According to [person]," as the *spine* of the post.
- Any structure where an external source drives the post and Sri reacts to it. Invert it: Sri's observation is the claim; the external fact is the support.
- Anecdote-led openings that read like a feature article ("It was a Tuesday in Mumbai when…").

The test: would a thinker writing his own conviction say this, or would a journalist reporting on someone else? Keep the first, cut the second.

## 10. Crypto-jargon translation list (for the non-crypto Indian reader)

The reader is smart but not crypto-native. On first use, either land the term in one plain clause or cut it. Never stack jargon.

**The one term that must always be taught: "on-chain."** The audience does not know what it means for a product to be on-chain, and the entire finance→DeFi reframe collapses if they don't. Never use "on-chain," "tokenized," or "runs on a public ledger" as if it were self-evident. Every post that leans on the idea must spend a sentence, in plain words, explaining that on-chain means the record of ownership and movement sits on a shared public ledger anyone can read and verify, instead of inside a single company's private books. Build the reframe on that explanation, not on the assumption the reader already has it.

A few common terms and how to land them:

- **DeFi** → "decentralised finance, finance that runs on open networks instead of through a single institution"
- **tokenization** → "turning an asset, a bond, a fund, a property, into something that can be held and traded digitally on an open network"
- **on-chain / settlement** → the reader does NOT know this term; teach it the first time, every post. Concretely: the record of who owns what and what moved when lives on a shared public ledger that anyone can read and check, instead of inside one company's private database that only it can see and edit. That is the whole point of the reframe, so spend a sentence making it real, e.g. "settled on a shared public ledger in seconds, a record anyone can read, rather than through layers of intermediaries over days inside systems only they can see"
- **custody / counterparty risk** → "the risk that the institution holding your money fails or misuses it"
- **smart contract** → "code that executes the agreement automatically, without a middleman deciding whether to honour it"
- **stablecoin** → "a digital token pegged to a currency like the dollar"
- **liquidity** → usually keep, but make sure context carries it ("how easily an asset can be bought or sold")
- **yield** → fine, the Indian finance reader knows this from fixed deposits and mutual funds; lean on that familiarity
- **RWA / real-world assets** → spell it out: "real-world assets, things like bonds, real estate, and funds, brought on-chain"

Lean on Indian finance familiarity the reader already has: fixed deposits, mutual fund minimums, demat accounts, UPI, GIFT City, RBI, SEBI. Bridge new ideas to these anchors.

Do NOT dilute the *technical* vocabulary into vagueness where precision is the point (custody means custody; counterparty means counterparty). Land it plainly once, then use it correctly. Accessibility is achieved by explaining, not by softening into mush.

## 11. Worked before/after examples

**Reporter → thought-leader**

Before (reporter): "I recently spoke with a founder building tokenized bond markets, and his story stopped me cold. He told me that most Indian investors will never touch a corporate bond. That made me think."

After (Sri): "Most Indian investors will never own a corporate bond directly. The minimum ticket size keeps them out, and the market was built that way. That is not an accident of demand. It is a design choice about who gets access."

**Jargon wall → accessible**

Before: "Tokenized RWAs settled on-chain via smart contracts remove counterparty risk and unlock fractional liquidity for retail."

After: "Bring a bond on-chain and two things change. It can be owned in small fractions, so the ticket size that locked most people out disappears. And it settles on a shared ledger in seconds, without trusting a single institution to hold the money and honour the trade."

**Tidy-bow close → open ending**

Before: "Tokenization will democratise finance. The future is on-chain, and it is inevitable."

After: "The technology to give far more people access to these markets largely exists. What is unsettled is whether the institutions that benefit from the current gatekeeping will let it in, or build the next set of gates."

**Hollow reframe → real reframe**

Before: "It's not about technology. It's about trust." (says nothing)

After: "That is not a technology gap alone. It is a market access gap." (corrects the diagnosis the reader was about to make)

**The episode plug (rare, strong correlation only, footer)**

Default is no plug. It attaches only when a recorded un# episode went deep on the *same thesis* the post argues, so a reader who liked the post finds the episode delivers. Adjacency fails. When it does fire, it sits after the post has ended on its open tension, separated by a line break, calm proper-case, no hype.

Good (only if the episode truly centred on this):
"Went deep into this exact mechanism with [Guest] of [Company] on the un# podcast, link in comments."

Bad (forced on a loose match, kills trust):
"As my guest explained on the podcast, this is why tokenization wins. Listen now!"

**The cautionary case.** A post about a lender being blind to his own position in a P2P blow-up is *about* trust and visibility. An episode where the guest, in passing, said crypto is "about trust, transparency, and incentives" is adjacent, not the same thesis. Plugging that episode promises the reader a deep treatment of the post's specific argument and the episode won't deliver it. The correlation has to be the episode's spine, not a line in it. When the match is only plausible, omit the plug and note it in the tail instead. No invented guests, no fabricated URL in the body; the link goes in the first comment.
