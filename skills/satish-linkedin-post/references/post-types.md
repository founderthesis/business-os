# Satish — Post Types

Each type has a skeleton, a length target, and one or two annotated exemplars taken verbatim from his published corpus. Read the type you're drafting before you write. The exemplars are the ground truth; the skeletons just name the moves.

---

## Type 1 — News-reaction (the most common type)
**Length:** 150-350 words. **When:** a fresh news event, deal, filing, or policy move that Satish can read in a non-obvious way.

**Skeleton:**
1. Hook (first two lines): lead with a recognizable name or a striking number — the name-based / McKinsey-style hook or the number hook (voice-rules §1, Tier 1/2). The news event usually supplies the famous anchor.
2. The fact, stated cleanly — what actually happened, with the verified number.
3. The non-obvious read — what most people miss, or the second-order point. Often a "First… Second…" or a two-column contrast.
4. The bridge to ownership/liquidity/talent — surfacing as the *explanation*, not the headline.
5. Aphoristic compression close.

**Exemplar (verbatim) — the BrowserStack post:**
> SaaS apocalypse? BrowserStack: hold my beer.
>
> Over the last year, BrowserStack has been pushing aggressively into AI-powered testing. At the same time, it announced a $125 million liquidity program for employees and investors.
>
> Two things stood out to me.
>
> First, who funded it.
> 👉 The company's own balance sheet.
> Not a new investor. Not a secondary fund. Not a pre-IPO liquidity round.
>
> Second, who it was designed for.
> 👉 Over 500 current and former employees. 👉 Early investors, including Accel.
>
> A company doesn't repeatedly create liquidity unless it's generating real cash.
> ...
> Because the value of equity isn't determined when it's granted.
> It's determined when people can actually do something with it.

*Why it works:* opens on a meme-y reframe, lands the verified number, runs the First/Second structure, and ownership emerges as the lesson in the last two lines. The close is a clean contrast.

**Exemplar (verbatim) — the HDFC post:**
> HDFC Bank's board just did something that tells you exactly who's been winning India's finance talent war. And it isn't the bank.
>
> It's Groww. It's CRED. It's Upstox. ...
>
> Last week, HDFC raised its RSU cap per employee by 67%. ...
>
> This isn't a routine comp tweak. It's a concession.
> ...
> But here's the uncomfortable truth for legacy finance: you can match RSU numbers on a spreadsheet. You can't manufacture the feeling of building something from zero.
> ...
> If HDFC matched your fintech offer rupee for rupee tomorrow, would you actually take it?

*Why it works:* news-anchored hook with a twist ("And it isn't the bank"), the clean reversal ("It's a concession"), the standing thesis stated freshly, and an earned specific reader question to close. (When drafting fresh, avoid "but here's the thing" — that exemplar's "here's the uncomfortable truth" is borderline; prefer a cleaner turn.)

## Type 2a — Episode story (full narrative)
**Length:** 250-400 words. **When:** a Built to Share episode contains a story or moment worth telling in full.

**Skeleton:**
1. Hook (first two lines): a scene open or juxtaposition that drops the reader into the moment — but get a recognizable name into those first two lines (the guest's famous customer, backer, school, or the well-known company/comparable). Lead with the most recognizable thing the story legitimately touches (voice-rules §1, Tier 1).
2. The story, told with concrete detail — names, numbers, the actual exchange. Quotes verbatim from the transcript.
3. The turn — what the story reveals.
4. The lesson. If ownership/liquidity genuinely fits, surface it in **one clean beat** — not a back half that restates it. Often the truer lesson is conviction, character, or a contrarian bet; let that be the lesson and keep ownership a light touch or absent (see the Wonderchef exemplar — ESOP never appears).
5. Close on the **strongest beat**, usually a quote or human turn, optionally with one short wrap-up line. Do not tack an equity compression onto the end if the post's power is elsewhere.
6. "Full conversation with [Guest] on built2share. Link in comments." — bare CTA, no name-tags or hashtag footer appended (link in comments, never in body).

**The forced-ESOP trap (read this before drafting an episode story).** The strongest temptation in this type is to take a great human story (a founder's conviction, a hard bet, a listing-day pop) and then over-explain the ownership angle until it dominates the back half. A real example of the failure: an ideaForge/Ankit Mehta draft told the trifecta story well — hardware, defense, and going public, with the line "If not us, then who will?" — and then spent four sentences restating that the ESOP pool was paper until the listing made it real. One ownership beat was earned; four buried the conviction line that should have closed the post. The fix: make the ownership point once (here, a single line that the listing is what turned paper into a real economic event), then close on "If not us, then who will?" with at most one wrap-up line.

**Exemplar (verbatim) — the Wonderchef / Zepto post:**
> A founder walked into Zepto with a ten-line Excel sheet and changed their mind about their own business. ...
>
> As the Zepto team put it, laughing: "Sir, quick commerce means quick. FMCG means fast moving."
>
> Wonderchef founder Ravi Saxena didn't argue with the belief.
>
> He asked them to be patient and look at a spreadsheet. ... Then he put a Nutriblend beside it. A ₹2,500 product. Even after a 20% discount, the math worked out to roughly ₹220 of profit on every unit, even at one sale a week.
>
> That made them sit up.
>
> Wonderchef now does over ₹100 crore of ARR on quick commerce alone.
>
> The rule everyone believed had been built around groceries. Ravi ran the math for appliances. The answer turned out to be very different.
>
> Listen in for more in our latest podcast.

*Why it works:* pure scene open, verbatim quote, concrete numbers, single-line landing ("That made them sit up."), and the lesson is about challenging a believed rule — ownership isn't even the surface subject. The CTA is bare and points to the episode.

**Exemplar (verbatim, condensed) — the Cashify women's-bathroom post:**
> A bill on women's reservation just failed in Parliament. A founder I spoke to recently didn't need a vote to solve it. ...
> his warehouse manager said: "We can't. We don't have a female bathroom." ...
> Nakul approved it. ...
> Today, that factory is 22% women. ...
> One founder with one bathroom did in 5 years what legislation hasn't managed in 30.
> ...
> You don't need a two-thirds majority to approve a bathroom.

*Why it works:* juxtaposition hook (Parliament vs one founder), verbatim quote, a hard number (22%), and a compression close that lands the whole thing in one line.

## Type 2b — Episode teaser (short)
**Length:** 40-110 words. **When:** announcing a new episode or pre-seeding one, without telling the full story.

**Skeleton:**
1. A one or two-line provocation or insight from the episode (the smallest interesting claim).
2. Name the guest and what you get into.
3. "New episode on built2share" / "Link in comments."

**Exemplar (verbatim):**
> If no one in your category has built what you're trying to build, who do you actually hire?
>
> In this episode I speak with Ravi Saxena, founder of Wonderchef ... about why none of his 22 senior leaders come from the kitchen appliances industry.
>
> We get into hiring for belief, why domain expertise can be a trap, how he reads candidates for cognitive flexibility ...
>
> Listen in. New episode on built2share

*Why it works:* a sharp question, a concrete hook (22 senior leaders, none from the industry), a tight list of what's covered, and a bare CTA.

## Type 3 — Thesis / Explainer
**Length:** 200-420 words. **When:** laying out a standing position, or teaching how a mechanic actually behaves.

**Skeleton:**
1. Hook (first two lines): a contrarian flip or a sharp question, ideally anchored to a recognizable name or number (voice-rules §1, Tier 1-3).
2. The common belief, named plainly.
3. The reality, with the plain-English mechanic translation where an instrument appears.
4. Why it matters — for employees, founders, the ecosystem.
5. Compression or quiet-forward-look close.

**Exemplar (verbatim) — the secondaries thesis:**
> An IPO is not the most common way employees make money from ESOPs. It's just the most visible one.
>
> Most ESOP conversations in India still revolve around: "When is the company going public?"
> But by the time that happens:
> - many employees have already left
> - exercise windows have expired
> - or the value was never realised
>
> The real story of ESOPs is not grants. It's liquidity.
>
> And increasingly, that liquidity is not coming from IPOs. It's coming from secondaries.
> ...
> ESOPs were never meant to stay illiquid forever. The market is finally catching up.

*Why it works:* contrarian reframe hook, the belief named, the mechanic broken down concretely, and a quiet forward-look close. **Caution:** this is one of Satish's standing theses — a new thesis post that just restates it is COVERED (see dedup reference). Only draft a thesis post when the angle is fresh.

**Exemplar (verbatim, condensed) — the ESOP-software explainer:**
> Why feature comparison tables are the worst way to pick ESOP software ...
> The difference is not in whether a feature exists. It's in how it behaves when things get messy.
> ...
> Ask this instead: "What breaks first when things get messy?"
> Features are table stakes. Behavior under complexity is the product.

*Why it works:* teaches a buying mistake, uses concrete examples (grant rounding, vesting edge cases), and closes on a clean compression.

## Type 4 — Data / Milestone
**Length:** 150-350 words. **When:** sharing platform data, a milestone, or a reflection (often year-end / FY close).

**Skeleton:**
1. Hook: the headline number, or a question the data answers.
2. Context — why now, what the data is.
3. The honest read — what's working, what isn't (Satish is candid about "still early").
4. The direction, stated without hype.

**Exemplar (verbatim, condensed) — the FY-close reflection:**
> India has created ESOP ownership at scale. The next question is: can it create ESOP wealth at scale?
> ... 350 million employee stock options. $3.5 billion in equity grants.
> ...
> ESOPs only work when three things are true:
> - The plan is well-designed
> - The employee actually understands it
> - There is a real path to liquidity
> Most plans get one of the three right. Very few get all three.
> ...
> We have to move beyond ownership creation to wealth creation. That's what we're building towards.
> A long way to go but the numbers above suggest the direction is right.

*Why it works:* question hook that frames the whole post, real platform numbers, an honest "most get one of three right", and a candid forward look. No hype.

## Type 5 — Operational (condolence / congratulations / hire / event)
**Length:** 40-160 words. **When:** a human moment — a tribute, a congratulations, a hiring post, an event note.

**Skeleton:** Say the human thing first. Keep it specific and warm. No equity sermon attached. If it's a hire, the role and why it matters can carry one line of Hissa's philosophy, but the post is about the person/moment.

**Exemplar (verbatim, condensed) — the hiring post:**
> Have you ever tried getting a flight refund on MakeMyTrip? ...
> Now imagine doing that while deciding whether to exercise your ESOPs before a liquidity window closes.
> ...
> At Hissa we have decided to do the opposite. Our customers don't want a bot. They want someone who has actually run an ESOP program before.
> ...
> That's the person we're hiring next. 👇

*Why it works:* even a hiring post opens on a relatable scene (the refund bot), makes the point about why human expertise matters, and only then points at the role. Ownership stays the emergent lesson.

---

## Cross-type length discipline
Modal Satish post is 200-350 words. If a draft exceeds ~400w and it isn't a full episode story or a milestone reflection, cut. Cuts almost always live in: restated points (use a single-line beat instead), setup that delays the thesis (his hook usually is the thesis or its setup), and adjective stacks.
