# Satish — Voice Rules

Everything here is drawn from Satish's published LinkedIn corpus. When a draft needs a hook, a close, or a check against an AI tell, this is the source. Read the sections the current draft actually needs rather than the whole file.

---

## 1. Opening hooks — the highest-leverage element

**Why the hook is the whole game (from the April 2026 team review of post analytics).** LinkedIn shows a new post to roughly 5% of followers first. If those readers read the **first two lines**, the algorithm pushes it to a much larger audience. The team's analysis found the posts that won did so on **impressions, not engagement** — people read the hook and moved on, and the impressions are what compounded. So the first two lines, before the "see more" fold, carry the entire post. Everything below the fold only matters if the hook earns the click.

What the review concluded works, in priority order:

### Tier 1 — the name-based hook (the tested winner)
Lead the first line with a **recognizable name**: a famous company, person, or institution. This is "non-topic-specific" by design — the famous name stops the scroll, the curiosity gap makes them read on, and the post only gets to ownership later. The viral outperformers leveraged names like McKinsey and Cashify. Use this first whenever the material gives you a recognizable anchor.

- **The McKinsey-style format** — open with a known institution's rule or practice, then pivot to your subject. *"McKinsey had a simple rule: up or out."* → then the contrast with the company in question. This is the specific template the team templated off; reach for it when there's an institutional practice to anchor on.
- **The big-name news anchor** — *"HDFC Bank's board just did something that tells you exactly who's been winning India's finance talent war. And it isn't the bank."* / *"Did Anthropic just kill private secondaries platforms?"*
- **The big-name juxtaposition** — *"Google made two founders look iconic. It also made people forget how fragile two can be."* / *"A $100M offer from Meta makes headlines. A $2.19M raise that ended in a real cash exit for every early employee does not."*
- **The big-name stakes line** — *"Sequoia Capital and Matrix offered him 8x the cash and 30x the stock. Visham couldn't match it."*

When the subject isn't itself famous, borrow a recognizable anchor that the story legitimately touches — the customer (the Indian Army), the school (IIT Bombay), the backer (Qualcomm, Accel), the comparable (the consensus "rule" the founder broke). The anchor must be real and load-bearing to the story, never a floating name-drop.

#### The recognizability test (apply to every hook)
Rank the named thing in your first two lines by how recognizable it is to a **cold reader** — a second-degree connection who does not follow Satish and has never heard of the subject. This matters because the algorithm's whole job is to push the post *past* the 5% who follow him to people who don't. To that cold reader:
- "McKinsey", "Google", "the Indian Army", "HDFC", "Sequoia", "IIT Bombay" — instantly recognizable. ✅ Strong anchor.
- "ideaForge", "Wonderchef", "Cashify", a founder's name — recognizable to the core VC/founder audience, mostly unknown to the cold reader. ⚠️ Weak anchor for reach.

If the subject company or founder is **not** a household name, do not lead with its name. Borrow the biggest recognizable name the story actually involves and lead with that instead. The subject's name comes *after* the hook has done its work.

#### Withhold the subject's name (default)
A hook that names an unfamiliar company/founder in line one spends the reader's attention on a name that means nothing to them. A hook that withholds it — "The drones the Indian Army flies on the border come from a company that..." — creates a curiosity gap (*which* company?) on top of the recognizable anchor. **Default to withholding the subject company name and founder name from the first two lines.** Reveal them in the next beat. This is a strong default, not an absolute rule: see the hook-gate mix in SKILL.md Step 5 (one of three options may name the subject directly, e.g. when the subject is genuinely well-known or the direct line is unusually strong).

### Tier 2 — the striking number
When there's a figure big or surprising enough to stop the scroll on its own, lead with it. High impressions came from number hooks too.
- *"By Series C, employees collectively own more equity than the founders who started the company."*
- *"At CoolIT Systems, more than 600 employees shared payouts averaging $240,000."*
- *"OpenAI employees sold $6.6B of equity..."*
- *"Meesho employees exercised ESOPs worth ~₹1,500 crore in just a few weeks."*

### Tier 3 — the contrarian flip
A widely-held read, then the flip. Works well, lower ceiling than a famous name unless the flip is genuinely surprising.
- *"Most people are reading the Infosys ESOP news wrong."*
- *"An IPO is not the most common way employees make money from ESOPs. It's just the most visible one."*

### Also in the toolkit (use when the material fits)
- **Scene / story open** — *"A founder walked into Zepto with a ten-line Excel sheet and changed their mind about their own business."* (Strong, but get a recognizable name into the first two lines — here, Zepto.)
- **Sharp question** — *"If no one in your category has built what you're trying to build, who do you actually hire?"*
- **Plain-language analogy** — *"Jitni chadar utni pair."* / the MakeMyTrip refund bot.

### Hook rules
- **Get a recognizable name or a striking number into the first two lines** — recognizable to a *cold reader*, not just to the core audience (apply the recognizability test above). If the subject isn't a household name, borrow a bigger one the story legitimately involves.
- **Default to withholding the subject's company/founder name** from the first two lines; lead with the borrowed anchor or the situation and reveal the subject in the next beat. One of the three hook options offered may break this and name the subject directly.
- **The hook is non-topic-specific.** It does not announce ESOPs. The famous name or number pulls the reader in; ownership emerges later (same discipline as §4).
- **Borrowed authority must be anchored** to a real named entity or event the story actually involves. No floating celebrity name-drops.
- **Never open on the equity instrument itself.**
- **Pick one hook pattern, don't blend two.**
- Verify any borrowed-authority fact (who the customer/backer is, what the institution's rule actually is) before it leads a post — a wrong anchor in line one is the most visible kind of error.

## 2. Closing moves

**A. Aphoristic compression (default)** — a short landing, often a contrast.
- *"Because the value of equity isn't determined when it's granted. It's determined when people can actually do something with it."*
- *"Secondaries are not dead. But shortcuts are going to get expensive."*
- *"You don't need a two-thirds majority to approve a bathroom."*

**B. The clean reversal** — *"This is not A. It is B."*
- *"This is not the end of secondaries. It is the end of pretending that private company shares can trade like public stocks."*

**C. Action triad-of-restraint** — used sparingly, only when it lands as plain advice, not flourish.
- *"Sell a little. Hold a little. Stay in the game."* (Note: this is the one place a three-beat is allowed, because it is concrete advice, not rhythmic decoration. Do not manufacture tricolons elsewhere.)

**D. Earned specific question** — a real question, never generic bait.
- *"If HDFC matched your fintech offer rupee for rupee tomorrow, would you actually take it?"*
- Banned: "What do you think? Drop your thoughts below."

**E. Quiet forward look** — names the direction without hype.
- *"ESOPs were never meant to stay illiquid forever. The market is finally catching up."*
- *"A long way to go, but the numbers suggest the direction is right."*

Default to A. Reach for D only when the question is genuinely specific and the reader could actually answer it.

**Choosing where to end: close on the strongest beat.** Before settling the close, find the single best line in the draft. If it's a quote or a human turn — "If not us, then who will?" — end on or near it, optionally with one short wrap-up line after. Do not bury a great line under an ownership compression just because the theme is ESOPs. The close should be the post's peak, not its thesis statement restated.

## 3. Structural moves

**The plain-English translation.** When the post touches an instrument, give one line a layperson understands. *"In plain English, it's a limited-time 'buyback window' where the company lets you swap your private paper for actual cash."* This is a deliberate, recurring Satish move — it makes him the explainer in the room.

**The two-column contrast.** He often sets two states against each other (US vs India, fintech vs legacy, grant vs liquidity) with parallel short lines. Keep the columns concrete; don't let them become abstract parallelism.

**The enumerated mechanic.** Short hyphen lists for the moving parts of a thing: *"- Grant by value - Rounding - Pool consistency"*. Used to show the messy reality under a clean label. Fine, because it's concrete and functional.

**The single-line landing.** A one-sentence paragraph that lands the turn: *"That made them sit up."* / *"This isn't a routine comp tweak. It's a concession."* Every post over 200 words should have at least one.

**First / Second framing.** For news posts with two observations: *"Two things stood out to me. First, who funded it. ... Second, who it was designed for."* Often paired with a 👉 to point at the answer.

## 4. The emergent-answer rule (load-bearing)

ESOPs / ownership / liquidity are the **lesson**, not the **headline**. The post opens on a human story, a news event, a number, or a talent decision. Ownership surfaces as the thing that explains it.

- The Wonderchef post opens on quick-commerce unit economics; ownership never even appears — the lesson is about challenging a believed rule. ✅
- The Cashify post opens on a women's bathroom and a P&L hit; the lesson is ownership-as-belief in people. ✅
- A post that opens "ESOPs are the best retention tool founders have" — has announced the topic instead of earning it. ❌ Rebuild.

If the brief hands you "write about ESOPs", your first job is to find the story that makes the point.

### Emergent ≠ forced ≠ belaboured (the counterweight)

Just as bad as leading with ESOP is **hammering it at the end**. Emergent means the ownership point lands in **one clean beat**, then stops. Watch for the back-half pile-up where the same idea gets said three or four ways:

> ❌ "...for years that ownership was paper. A grant only becomes wealth when there's a way to turn it into cash. The day the stock listed was the day their ownership stopped being a line in an offer letter. The listing is what let the team finally hold it."

That is one point, four times. It bloats the post and buries the best line. The fix:

- **One beat.** Say the ownership thing once, plainly, and move on. If two sentences make the same point, cut one.
- **Sometimes barely there.** If the story's real power is conviction, character, or a contrarian bet, let *that* carry it. Ownership can be a single light touch — or, like Wonderchef, absent. Don't manufacture the connection to satisfy the theme.
- **Don't let it take the close.** If the post has a strong quote or human turn, end there (see §2). Don't tack an equity compression onto a post whose strength is elsewhere.

Default to under-explaining ownership. Satish's readers know what he does; the restraint is the voice.

## 5. Vocabulary

**Use-words / registers Satish actually reaches for:**
liquidity, ownership, exercise, vesting, tender offer, secondary, buyback, lock-in, RSU, cap table, dilution, grant, realised, capital allocation, compounding, alignment, retention, conviction, signal, the math, unit economics, operator, "what stood out to me", "what I find fascinating", "the real story is".

**Ban-words / phrases (never Satish):**
"game-changer", "paradigm shift", "in today's fast-paced world", "at the end of the day", "needle-mover", "synergy", "leverage" (as a verb), "unlock potential" (as filler), "thrilled to" (outside genuine congratulations), "hot take", "unpopular opinion", "let that sink in", "here's the truth no one tells you", "Founders, listen up".

**Register notes:**
- First person for reflection ("I sat with Nakul last week", "what stayed with me").
- Confident, no hedging stacks ("perhaps", "arguably", "might be considered").
- Plain over ornate. He'd rather be clear than clever.

## 6. AI-tells strip table

Scan every draft against this before output.

| Pattern | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Em-dashes (—) | Satish uses **zero** in his posts. A dead AI giveaway. | Replace with spaced hyphens ( - ), periods, or line breaks. |
| "It's not just X — it's Y" closer/turn | Classic AI cadence. Satish uses the clean *"This is not A. It is B."* instead. | Rewrite as the clean two-sentence declarative, or cut. |
| Manufactured tricolon (three balanced abstract phrases for rhythm) | Reads as AI reaching for profundity. His genuine three-beats are concrete advice, not decoration. | Cut to two beats, or make it concrete. Only keep a triad if it's plain instruction like "Sell a little. Hold a little. Stay in the game." |
| "But here's the thing" / "And here's the kicker" / "Plot twist:" | Manufactured pivots. He lets the next sentence carry the turn. | Cut the signpost; let the line land on its own. |
| Generic "What do you think? Drop your thoughts below" | Engagement bait. | Use an earned specific question, or an aphoristic close. |
| "Thrilled to announce" / "Excited to share" as a reflex | LinkedIn cliché outside real congratulations. | Lead with the news or the story directly. |
| Adjective stacks ("genuinely transformative, deeply powerful, world-class") | Over-engineered; his register is sparer. | One adjective or none. |
| Hedging stacks ("perhaps", "arguably", "in some ways could be seen as") | AI playing safe. He states claims. | Strip the hedge; state the claim. If it doesn't hold without the hedge, cut the claim. |
| Symmetric "Not because A. But because B." overused as a flourish | He uses it occasionally and earned; AI overuses it. | Keep at most one per post, and only when it's the actual logic, not decoration. |
| Stacked emoji bullets (✅✅✅) or rocket emojis as section breaks | He uses the odd functional 👉👇, never decoration stacks. | Strip. Use a single-line paragraph for emphasis. |
| Unicode bold (𝗯𝗼𝗹𝗱) for emphasis | Appears in a couple of older reposts but not his own voice; reads as formatting trickery. | Strip. Use rhythm and line breaks for emphasis. |
| Vague quantities ("many employees", "thousands of startups", "a lot of founders") | Not specific enough for Satish. | Name the number or the named example. |
| "In summary" / "To wrap up" / "Key takeaways:" | A post ends on a compression, not a recap. | Cut. End on the aphoristic landing. |
| A URL in the post body for the podcast | His convention is link in comments. | Replace with "Link in comments" (or the relevant CTA line). |

## 7. Emoji and formatting conventions

- **Emoji:** sparing and functional only. 👉 to point at an answer, 👇 to point down at a link/CTA, ❤️♣️ to mark the Equity Deck card series, 😁 in a genuinely light moment. Default to none. Never decorative, never stacked.
- **Hashtags:** default to **none**. Akshay's standing preference is clean posts without hashtags. Add them only if he asks for a specific post.
- **Name-mentions / tags:** default to **none**. Do not append a "Guest · Hissa · #tags" footer at the foot of the post — Akshay strips this routinely. Mention a person by name only inside the body where it's part of the story (e.g. "I sat with Nakul last week"), not as a tag block at the end. Add a tag only on request.
- **Line breaks:** generous. White space between short paragraphs is the house style. No dense blocks.

## 8. The final check

Read the draft once more and ask: does this sound like a senior operator who noticed something real and decided to say it plainly, or like content built to perform? If the latter — lower the volume, cut the cleverness, add a specific number or a named person, and let ownership stay the lesson rather than the announcement.
