# Editorial rules, voice and design

Read this at Phase 5, before writing a word of the document, and again before
presenting. SKILL.md carries the process and the section order. This file carries
everything about how the writing and the design have to behave. Where this file
and a build file disagree, this file wins and the build file is stale.

---

## What the show is about

**The show belongs to the guest's world, not the client's product.** Everything else
in this file is downstream of that sentence, and it is the rule a run is most likely
to break, because the client will hand over the other answer and it will sound
reasonable.

### What the client is buying

They are buying the room. One hour with a senior person they could not otherwise get
in front of, in a setting where they are not selling and the other person is talking
about their own work. Ten minutes of surface conversation at a conference is the
thing it replaces.

Audience is a second-order benefit. Evangelism is not a benefit at all.

### The trap

Almost every client, asked what the show should be about, describes their own
category. A lending software company proposes a show about lending technology. A
company selling immersive labs proposes a show about immersive learning. It is the
obvious answer, it is what their marketing team would say, and it quietly destroys
the thing they are paying for.

Three reasons it fails:

- **The guests stop saying yes.** A senior person invited onto a vendor's show about
  the vendor's category knows exactly what the hour is for. Their communications team
  knows too. The invitation that works is one about the guest's own work.
- **It caps the guest list at the client's own market.** A show about lending software
  can only book people who care about lending software. A show about how India lends
  can book a former RBI deputy governor.
- **The audience is the wrong one.** A category show reaches people already shopping.
  The room is worth more than the traffic.

### The test

Two questions, and the subject has to pass both:

1. Would a senior person in the ICP's world give an hour to this subject **if the
   client did not exist**?
2. Would they watch an episode someone else recorded?

Two noes means it is a category brief in disguise. Rewording does not fix it; going
back to what the ICP argues about among themselves does.

### Where the client shows up

Not in the subject and not in the episodes. They show up in four places, and that is
enough:

- They convene it. Their name presents the show and their leader asks the questions.
- They own it. Channel, handles, recordings, masters, ad account.
- They choose the guest list, which is the target account list.
- Their sales team gets a real relationship with every guest, and a conversation to
  reference afterwards.

**Product stays off the air.** Say it in the document, under what the host gets asked
for. It is also what gets a guest's communications team to approve the booking.

### Handling the client who pulls it back

They will, and usually in the second round: could we do an episode on how companies
are solving this, could the guest talk about the problem we fix, could we get a
customer on to talk about the deployment. Answer it in chat rather than in the
document, and answer it with the mechanism:

> The moment an episode is about the problem you solve, the next invitation reads as
> a vendor asking for an hour, and the people you actually want stop replying. The
> show earns its keep by being worth their time on its own.

On the iXR Labs pitch the client rejected an employability-first theme because it
"makes marketing content for the college rather than for iXR". That instinct is
understandable and it points the wrong way: a show that makes something valuable for
the guest's world is exactly what gets iXR into the room. Note the objection, keep the
subject, and make the case once.

### What this means downstream

- **Themes** are no longer a section of the pitch (cut October 2026). The working
  names and their taglines carry the show's direction.
- **The episode spine** is built around a number that moved in the guest's business,
  which is already guest-centric. Keep it that way.
- **The show name** belongs to the guest's profession. Full rules in `naming.md`.
- **The guest list** is a target account list, and it only works if the invitation is
  worth accepting.

## The one idea every version sells

One recorded conversation creates three outcomes: pipeline, inbound, content.

- **Pipeline.** The guest is a buyer. An hour on the show turns a cold target into
  a warm relationship, with no pitch.
- **Inbound.** The guest's peers watch the episode and reach out.
- **Content.** Every episode becomes weeks of assets across the client's channels.

Every section ladders up to one of these. If a section does not, question why it is
there. This is a fully managed production and business-development offer, not a
marketing add-on.

## The opener

The first thing on the page under the title, in every Route B pitch. **Short, and one
pitch only.** Four short paragraphs, roughly 75 to 90 words all in.

**Why it changed (October 2026, Drizz).** The September shape stacked a three-beat
on-ramp on top of a four-block opener: their problem, why their route tops out, the
proposal, then the buying committee, the access argument as a pull quote, what an
episode is and the ownership line. Six paragraphs and about 270 words before the
reader reached anything he could react to. Akshay's read: an info dump, and we lose
the reader there. Cut to about a third, it lands.

### The four paragraphs

1. **The proposal, with the host named.** The word podcast, the client's name, and
   the host **by name** in the first sentence. Where the founders have not decided
   who hosts, name the candidates: "hosted by co-founders Yash Varyani or Asad
   Abrar". Then what one episode is, in one sentence: an hour with [role] at a
   [kind of company] the client wants to work with.
2. **It opens doors.** One or two sentences. The person who ignores a cold email
   usually says yes to an hour talking about their own work.
3. **It builds the client's name.** One or two sentences. Guests share their episode
   with their own networks, so every recording puts the client in front of the
   community as the company hosting these conversations. This is the thought
   leadership and brand positioning half of the pitch, and it is the half the
   September openers left out.
4. **Ownership.** One line. The channel, the recordings and everything published
   belong to the client.

### Rules

- **Two jobs, nothing else.** Door opening, and brand position in the community.
  Anything that is not one of those two (the buying committee, why their current
  channel tops out, the comms-team argument, the format) goes later in the document
  or nowhere.
- **Name the host by name, at the very start.** "A Drizz co-founder" was flagged as
  not clearly saying who hosts.
- **No pull quote, no on-ramp, no lead class.** The opener is short enough not to
  need visual texture.
- **Sourced, never invented.** Every fact in it (the market they are going after, who
  the guests are) comes from the transcript or verified research.
- **No universal quantifiers.** "Usually says yes" survives a sceptical founder.
  "Always says yes" does not.

### Worked example: Drizz, October 2026

> A podcast for Drizz, hosted by co-founders Yash Varyani or Asad Abrar. Each episode
> is an hour with an engineering leader at a US app company Drizz wants to work with.
>
> It opens doors. A VP of engineering who ignores a cold email will usually say yes to
> an hour talking about their own work.
>
> It builds Drizz's name. Guests share their episode with their own networks, so every
> recording puts Drizz in front of the engineering community as the company hosting
> these conversations.
>
> The channel, the recordings and everything published belong to Drizz.

### Route A

One line on where this came from and what was agreed, then a short Overview. "You and
Akshay settled the shape of this on the call in September. This is what it looks like
built." Sourced from the transcript.

## What an episode is

Its own section, straight after the working names. **An episode is a broad, open
conversation with the guest about their own work, never an episode built around one
number.** A podcast invitation means the host wants a wider conversation with this
person, and a guest says yes to talking about themselves.

One sentence of framing (open conversation, no product pitch from the guest or the
client), then a short list of the ground it usually covers, adapted to the show:

- the career path that got them here
- what worked, and what they learnt from the calls that went wrong
- their take on the show's subject (for Drizz: AI writing code, and how they keep
  quality up as their team ships faster)
- how their thinking has changed over the last year

Then one line on the prep document the host gets before every recording.

**Retired (October 2026):** "each episode is built around one number that moved in
the guest's business". Akshay: episodes can't be about one number.

## What the sales team can use

Its own section, after "What one recording becomes" and before Investment. It is a
benefit, so it does not belong in the measurement section, and it is not a
deliverable, so it does not belong in the content grid.

The argument: the episodes do a second job that has nothing to do with the
audience, because every conversation arms the people already selling. Four
bullets, bold lead-in each:

- **Names to drop, from what aired.** "When I was talking to the CTO of [peer
  company] about this" opens a conversation that a cold email does not, and every
  episode adds one more. Off-mic stays off-mic: the line works for what was published
  and nothing else, and a seller quoting something a guest said before the recording
  started costs the show the next three bookings.
- **Objections in a buyer's own words.** Guests say out loud why they hesitated.
  The team gets the counter-arguments from a peer rather than from marketing. This
  is where the verbatim quotes from research section 3 land.
- **Proof without a case study.** A clip of someone else describing the problem
  lands harder than a vendor describing the solution, and it is already being
  produced.
- **A reason to reach out.** Sending a prospect the episode featuring their direct
  peer is a warm touch that asks for nothing.

Three things to hold to. The reference is always that the client talked to someone
about this problem, never a suggestion that the guest is a customer. The section stays
four bullets: it is a bonus argument, and a bonus argument that runs long starts to
look like the main one. And it closes on the honesty line, that all of this comes out
of assets post-production and amplification already deliver, so it adds no scope and
no cost.

**No selling off the guest's back.** Whatever the section promises, the follow-up goes
through whoever owns that account, a clear month after release, and is never framed as
podcast follow-up. Say it in one clause here or in the measurement section. It is the
rule that keeps guests accepting the second time you ask, and a client who skips it
burns the list faster than any content decision could.

## One listener, one arena

Before the themes, the names or a single guest, the document has to be able to finish
this sentence: **this is the show for [one person], about [one arena].**

A show for four audiences is a show for none. The audience section still lists the
groups who listen, because a client wants to see the spread, but one of them is the
listener the show is built for and the document says which. Everything downstream
tests against it: a theme that does not serve that person is a strand rather than the
lead, and a guest who does not speak to that person needs a reason to be in the list.

Two things make the arena worth owning:

- **It is narrow enough to be ownable.** "How the best consumer companies build
  playbooks that scale" fits a hundred shows. "Selling through the world's fifty
  million small shops" fits one, and the client is already in it.
- **The client has a real claim to convene it.** The arena should be somewhere their
  work already puts them, so hosting reads as natural rather than as marketing.

Where a category has a name that practitioners actually use, use it. Where it has an
academic term with a person attached, that person is usually available and is one of
the best early guests in the list.

**Lead with the show, not the vendor.** "Presented by [Client]" reads as an industry
show; "[Show] by [Client]" reads as marketing, and the difference decides whether a
multinational's comms team lets an executive accept. The client still owns every
credit line, the description, the handles and the share kit, so nothing is given up.
Say it once, in the opener.

## Naming the show

**The rules live in `references/naming.md`. Read that file before writing a single
candidate.** It carries the four tests, the shapes that work, the shapes that have
been rejected with the actual names, and the generation procedure.

The short version, because it is the part most often got wrong: the show belongs to
the guest's profession, not to the client's product. A name that a senior person who
would never buy from the client still wants to appear on is the bar. Names that prove
you know the client's trade, names that state a thesis, and names that lock the client
into a segment or a vertical have all been rejected in live pitches.

What stays here, because it is about the section rather than the name:

- **Three names, all short.** Three or four words beats a sentence. Three is
  deliberate: two is thin and a longer list reads as a brainstorm and makes the client
  do the editing you were paid for.
- **One line per name on the promise it makes to a listener**, which is what turns a
  naming discussion from taste into a decision. Write the promise, not a description
  of the words: "promises method, which is what a guest thinks they are being asked
  for", not "evokes expertise".
- **A bare name may need a descriptor beside it.** Most good short names are already a
  car, a film or a game in search results, so the title line can carry the name plus
  what the show is: "[Name]: the bank engineering show". Use it where the name alone
  would not survive a search, and leave it off where the name is already clear.
- **Check the plural.** Where the name describes the people in the chair, the plural
  form turns it into a compliment you pay the guest. "We would like to feature you as
  a [Name]" is a better invitation than anything else in the outreach email.
- **Check whether the client already owns a name, and whether an old feed is still
  live.** A show name they already hold settles this section in one line. An abandoned
  feed with zero episodes on Apple and Spotify is the first thing a careful guest's
  team finds, so it gets repointed or retired before launch and that belongs in Phase
  0 of the roadmap.
- **Never write a name as settled.** "Working titles, settled together in the first
  fortnight" appears once, in the section intro.
- **The page carries an add-your-own-name box.** A client who writes a name has bought
  into the show. Expect their name to beat yours, and say so if it does.

## No themes section

**Killed (October 2026).** The pitch carries no "Potential themes of the show"
section, no theme cards and no add-your-own-theme box. The working names already
carry a tagline each, and the tagline does the job a theme card was doing. "Working
names" is a main section heading (an h2), not a sub-label. Do not refer to themes,
strands or threads anywhere else in the document.

## Credibility

**One place only: Why us, at the close.** Everything in it comes from
`references/credentials.md`, nothing written from memory.

**Retired (October 2026):**
- The cover strip under the title ("Six years. Four shows. Two million views."). A
  cold reader meets numbers about a company that has not been introduced. Akshay: the
  credibility numbers don't belong in the subheading, they make no sense there.
- The credibility line above the guest roadmap. The roadmap opens straight on
  Phase 0 with no preamble. The Citi and Franklin Templeton names moved into the un#
  card in Why us.

**Credibility is stated in facts a reader can check, never in adjectives.** No
superlatives, no "award-winning", no "trusted by". Guests on our shows are never our
clients. And never claim the discipline itself: a document that protests its own
honesty invites the reader to go looking.

## Why us

The closing section of every pitch, after "What we need from [Client]" and before the
closing line. It answers whether these people can actually run a show.

**The order, top to bottom (October 2026):**

1. **The numbers band first.** Five figures, one row: **4** shows, **2M+** YouTube
   views, **150K+** subscribers, **270K** LinkedIn followers, **1,000+** hours
   recorded. The reader sees scale before reading a word.
2. **The origin paragraph**, verbatim from `credentials.md` ("Founder Thesis started
   as a passion project..."). Short sentences, said the way Akshay would say it.
3. **Four show cards.** Each card: the host line small and grey, the show name as a
   link to its YouTube channel, then ONE line of guest companies separated by ⬩,
   led by a label that says these are guests:
   - Founder Thesis: "Featuring founders of:" Info Edge ⬩ TeamLease ⬩ Zeta ⬩ IndiaMART ⬩ ...
   - un# (Unhashed): "Featuring senior leaders of:" Citi ⬩ Franklin Templeton ⬩ Circle ⬩ Bitwise ⬩ Zodia Custody
   - Human by Design: "Featuring:" Schneider Electric's Chief AI Officer ⬩ Jürgen Schmidhuber, whose work underpins modern AI
   - Built to Share: "Featuring founders of:" CarDekho ⬩ ideaForge ⬩ Scripbox ⬩ Cashify ⬩ IDfy
4. **The closing line:** "Your total commitment is three to six hours a month. The
   rest is ours. More at myunnati.com."

**Why the cards changed.** The September cards were two sentences of prose per show
("Access. Sandy Kaul, head of innovation at Franklin Templeton..."). Akshay: if I am
reading, I will skip everything below the show names. Name-dropping companies in short
phrases is what a skimming reader actually takes in. Without the "Featuring..." label
the company list read as clients or partners rather than guests, so the label is
mandatory.

**Rules that still hold:** all four shows every time, Founder Thesis first, un# stays
in even when it feels off-topic, every show name links to its channel on the page and
in the Word export, choose the companies a reader in this prospect's world would
recognise, spell every name per `credentials.md`, never estimate a number, no client
logos, testimonials or awards.

## The covering email

The pitch does not arrive on its own. The last thing every run produces is the email
that carries the link, written to be pasted into Gmail as plain text. It is short,
one idea per paragraph, and it does not try to do the document's job.

### The shape

```
Subject: A podcast for [Company]

Hi [First name],

As we discussed, I would love to work with you on a podcast for [Company].

A podcast that is your own IP, and you or someone from the leadership team hosts.

Here's a reference podcast: [linked name of a real show in their world].

The goal is to get you in the room in front of the right set of people by
inviting them to an intimate long form conversation with you, instead of ten
minutes of surface conversation at an event.

The bonus is that each episode is a gold mine of unique insights that can be
published in a dozen formats: thought leadership posts, newsletters, short
videos, carousels, infographics.

Here's an outline of what the podcast could look like, how it would run, whom
it could feature, and what it would cost: [pitch link]
```

### Rules

- **"As we discussed" only if a call happened.** Where there was no call, the opening
  changes to how the introduction came about, or to the reason for writing now. On the
  Finezza pitch nobody had ever spoken to Krishnan, and that line would have been the
  first thing he noticed. Check the transcript before writing it.
- **The reference podcast is the one piece of real work in the email.** It comes from
  the competing and adjacent shows found in Phase 2b, and it has to be a show aimed at
  this prospect's own market, ideally run by a competitor or an adjacent vendor. Link
  it by name. It does the hardest job in the whole email, which is making a podcast
  feel like a normal thing companies in their category already do. Where the research
  found nothing, say so in chat rather than reaching for a famous show from another
  industry: a generic reference is worse than none.
- **Six or seven paragraphs, one line or two each.** White space is the format. A
  paragraph that runs to four lines belongs in the document, not the email.
- **No pricing, no guest names, no theme.** All three live behind the link, and an
  email that summarises the document gives the reader a reason not to open it.
- **The last line is the link and what is behind it**, in the prospect's terms: what
  it could look like, how it would run, who it could feature, what it would cost. That
  sentence is doing the work of a call to action without being one.
- **Plain text, no formatting.** It is a founder writing to a founder. No bold, no
  bullets, no signature block beyond the usual.
- **Say "your own IP" early.** Ownership is the thing that separates this from a
  sponsorship or a guest slot, and it is the fastest reassurance in the email.

### Route A

Where the idea is already sold, the email is shorter still. The first two paragraphs
collapse into one that says what was agreed, and the reference podcast comes out
entirely, because a client who has decided to do this does not need proof the format
exists. The link and its description stay.

## Editorial rules

Hard-won. Do not relearn them.

- **Never write an unagreed decision as settled.** If the show name, the themes or
  the audience were not actually discussed with the client, the document says so:
  "working title", "a starting point rather than a settled plan", "we would settle
  this with you in the first fortnight". Give the user the options separately in
  chat to take back to the client.
- **A proposed guest list is not the same as a settled one.** Naming people under
  a roadmap is plainly a proposal and reads as one. Writing a theme as though it
  was agreed is a misrepresentation. Keep the distinction.
- **Watch for orphans when a section is renamed.** "A decision on which direction
  leads" survived a rename from "Three directions it could take" and stopped
  making sense. When a heading changes, grep the document for the old noun.
- **No absolutes about human behaviour.** "None of them take cold calls." "All of
  them will give ninety minutes." "Every CFO is drowning." A founder reads
  universal quantifiers as marketing and discounts the paragraph around them.
  Cutting them shortens the document and makes it more believable at once.
- **Write as if the document will be forwarded to everyone it describes.** Never
  call a buying group gatekeepers, blockers, or the people who stop deals. If a head
  of data ever reads that sentence, the show is dead in that account. Describe
  responsibilities and concerns instead.
- **Never describe a named guest as someone the client wants to sell to.** It
  contradicts the no-pitch promise made two lines above it, and the document names
  those people and can be forwarded to them. Use "wants to work with". The commercial
  framing is not lost: it lives in "these are the buyers" under Who it is for, and in
  "every name here is a target account" in Phase 2, neither of which is attached to a
  human being by name. This shipped once, two lines under a sentence promising the
  episode has no pitch in it, and it is two defects in one clause.
- **Never argue against part of what you are selling.** "The episode is the
  by-product." "Views are the wrong scoreboard." "Downloads are the wrong
  scoreboard." Pushed one step too far these tell the client that something they
  said they wanted is worthless. Rank the secondary benefit below the primary one
  instead of dismissing it, and keep the explanation value of a published
  conversation in the document, because most founders want it.
- **Mark predictions as targets.** Phase headings carry episode ranges rather than
  dates, and one sentence says it is the shape being aimed at rather than a
  guarantee. Name the review gate, after the first six published
  episodes. This keeps all the persuasive force and removes the liability.
- **Four numbers in measurement, no more.** Guests who turn into a commercial
  conversation within ninety days. How people respond to the cold invitations. How
  many further introductions the guests make. Engagement on LinkedIn across the
  posts, the newsletter and the short videos, framed as who is responding rather
  than as reach, because that is where the right people surface months before they
  reply to anything. Four numbered rows, each one sentence.
- **Track audience from episode one, reported separately.** Views, retention and
  subscribers get measured from the first episode and shown apart from the
  commercial numbers, which stay primary. Hiding the audience numbers until month
  six reads as avoidance, and a client who asked for reach will notice.
- **No intro paragraph in "How we measure it".** The heading goes straight into the
  four numbered rows. "A podcast appearance is not a lead..." was cut in September
  and again in October 2026 as wordy and indirect. Under the rows, one line only:
  views, retention and subscribers are tracked from the first episode and reported
  separately. No "titles are current as of..." line anywhere on the page.
- **Let the estimate column do the arithmetic.** The Investment table carries a live
  Estimate column and subtotals. Do not write a batch total into the prose: the reader
  configures it, and a fixed total under a modular table invites an argument about
  assumptions. The total goes to the user in chat instead. In the rare Word-only
  pitch, give one total for the episode batch and state its assumptions.
- **The page is the deliverable.** Do not build a standalone docx unless the user asks
  for one. The page's export carries the guests the client kept and the budget they
  configured, which a statically built file cannot, and a second build is a second
  thing to keep in sync. This also retires the old page-length rules: the page has no
  page count to win.
- **One line on ownership**, where it belongs. Channel, handles, raw recordings,
  edited masters and the ad account all sit with the client. It goes in the opener,
  not under the price table. Twenty-five words, and it closes the loudest objection
  nobody says out loud.
- **Know which document you are writing.** A pitch earns the meeting and is allowed
  conviction and compression. Turnaround times, revision rounds, approval windows
  and cancellation terms belong in the SOW, and one line saying so is enough.
  Reviewers will push contract precision into the pitch. Take the verifiable
  corrections and leave the governance tables out.
- **Never explain the client's company back to them.** No stat strip of their
  numbers, no recital of their customers or what their product does. Give the space
  to what they still have to decide. Route B's opener describes their buying
  problem, which is the opposite: it proves you listened.
- **Verified figures only.** No invented numbers, no rounding up, no borrowed stats
  you cannot stand behind. If a number has to be there for the document to make
  sense, say in chat which number was picked rather than sourced.
- **Carry forward confidentiality constraints.** Check the transcript and project
  memory for anything the client asked to keep off the record, including figures
  they asked to scrub from a recording. Say in chat that the constraint was
  applied, so it does not read as an omission.
- **No redundancy.** Each fact lives in exactly one section. Business and category
  in the opener, sequence in the roadmap, current numbers in Investment. Delete
  every second mention. The guest names are the target accounts, so there is no
  target account section.
- **Soft timing only.** "First fortnight", "from week two, then ongoing", "from
  launch". Never day-level precision: it invites disappointment over a schedule
  nobody agreed. Reviews are fortnightly, and appear once in the document.
- **Do not annotate the content grid with line items.** An earlier rule made the
  grid carry a sentence mapping each row to the line item that pays for it. It
  reads as a disclaimer in the middle of the best section, and the Investment table
  already says what each line covers. Keep the grid clean. The one thing that must
  still hold is the next rule.
- **Never describe as included anything the pricing table charges for.** Guest
  sourcing and outreach are a paid line, charged only on guests we source. Guest
  research, the prep dossier and guest engagement either side of the recording are
  included on every booking, whoever sourced it. Say which is which in the same
  sentence, in Investment. The same test applies to any Optional line: if a
  narrative section implies it is standard, one of the two is wrong.
- **Setup and post-production are not optional.** Do not offer them as toggles in
  the estimator or tag them Optional in the card. A pitch that lets the buyer switch
  off the thing that makes the show exist is not a pitch, it is a menu.
- **Frame paid promotion as a distribution test.** A targeted test to put episodes
  in front of the named cohort, with paid results reported separately from organic,
  and never a success metric. Do not argue that ads make a new channel look busy,
  because published episodes are what do that and a reader will say so.
- **Ground every count in contracted scope.** The asset count is whatever
  post-production plus amplification actually delivers, roughly twenty. Never a
  round marketing figure. If the client wants a bigger number, widen the
  amplification scope rather than change the claim.
- **Depth over volume.** Do not headline a content-multiplication engine. The
  derived assets are part of production, never the reason to do the show.
- **Positive framing.** Never demean the client or the guests. Not "someone you'd
  struggle to get a meeting with". Say the access value positively.
- **Never reopen a decision the document has settled.** The show is always owned by
  the client, and the opener says so. Nothing later may ask them to decide whether
  it carries their identity.
- **When a rule removes something, sweep every file for it.** Contradictions live
  in pairs, and they live in two build files: `build_concept_note.js` and
  `build_pitch_page.html`. Change both, every time.

## Handling a critique of a finished pitch

Reviews arrive, from the client, a colleague, or another model. They are useful and
they are not a brief.

- **Take everything checkable.** Wrong titles, stale facts, arithmetic, internal
  contradictions, and anything the document promises that the price list charges
  for. These hold up almost every time.
- **Weigh everything tonal against your own read of the room.** Commitment level,
  how much hedging to add, how much scope detail to include, whether to shrink the
  guest list. A reviewer with no stake in the deal will optimise for defensibility
  and leave you with a document that sells nothing.
- **Answer the critique before rewriting anything.** Say what you agree with and
  what you do not, and propose the smallest fix for each. A critique acted on
  silently produces a second document nobody asked for.
- **Pay for additions with cuts.** Name what comes out before agreeing what goes
  in, and report the net word count. A pitch that grows with every review round is a
  pitch nobody finishes reading.
- **Rebuild from the build script**, never by patching the .docx, and re-render the
  PDF and look at every page afterwards.

## Voice

- No em-dashes. Use commas, colons, full stops.
- Plain and human. No wordplay headings, no punchy fragments, no casual filler, no
  manifesto lines.
- Sentence-case headings that say what the section is.
- Specifics over adjectives. "Two to three horizontal clips per episode", not
  "premium, broadcast-grade content".
- Numbers live in the tables or in plain prose, never in decorative boxes.
- Frame from strength: "the next step is", not "what's missing is".
- Short, varied paragraphs. Three long ones in a row is where a reader leaves.
- Restraint is the premium signal. Clean, spare, confident.

Banned words: delve, leverage, unlock, robust, seamless, elevate, commanding,
definitive, pioneer, dominate, rebellion, and anything brochure-like. No rhetorical
triples. No "it's not X, it's Y".

## Design: the Word document

The house look comes from `build_concept_note.js`. If that file is ever lost, these
are the tokens:

- Calibri throughout. Body 11pt (docx size 22). Section headings 15pt (size 30)
  bold in the head colour with a thin rule beneath. Title 20pt (size 40). Kicker
  9.5pt (size 19) letterspaced in the muted colour. Table cells 10 to 11pt. Only the
  RECOMMENDED and OPTIONAL tag labels go smaller, at 8pt. 11pt is the minimum for
  all body text, bullets and table cells, because these get read on phones.
- Ink `20242B`, head `17365D`, muted `5A5F68`, rules `C9CED5`, band fill `EDF1F6`.
- Page margins: top 1080, bottom 1000, left and right 1200 twips. Usable width is
  9840 twips.
- **Every table sets `columnWidths` explicitly in twips at the Table level**, with a
  matching `width` in DXA on every cell and `layout: TableLayoutType.FIXED`. A
  percentage width with no column widths collapses the columns into slivers of
  vertical letters in Word, while looking fine in a PDF render. This applies to the
  build script and to the in-page export equally. In the script every table goes
  through the `table()` helper so the three can never drift apart.
- Pricing table: the header row repeats across page breaks, section groupings are
  full-width shaded band rows, tags sit as small caps beneath the item name, and the
  one-time setup is a row in the table rather than a section of its own.
- The content engine is a bordered three-by-four grid.
- **Length.** Five to six pages, under 2,200 words. The roadmap is the longest
  section and it earns that: it is what wins Route B. An earlier target of four
  pages and 1,700 words predates the roadmap and is no longer achievable with a
  full guest list. If the document is running past six pages, cut from the themes
  and the opener, never from the guest angles.

## Design: the HTML page

Built from `build_pitch_page.html` and published with the Artifact tool, private
until the user shares it. Call the Artifact tool with action "capabilities" before
publishing, because the page declares the `downloads` capability. The file must be a
complete HTML document with a doctype, or publish is rejected.

- **The house palette is the myunnati.com palette.** Ground `#F7F0E4`, panel
  `#FFFDF8`, band `#F1E7D5`, sunk `#FBF6EC`, ink `#17161A`, muted `#6E6860`, faint
  `#928B80`, red `#DE0A26`, soft red `#FCE9EB`, rules `#E3D9C7`. Plus Jakarta Sans.
  Light only, with `color-scheme: light` and a single deliberate theme. Do not ship
  a dark variant of a document that exists to look like the company's website.
- **One column, 740 to 760px.** Prose in a narrow column inside a wide card grid
  produces lumps of whitespace wherever a list of names appears. One column, all the
  way down, with cards that fill it.
- **Break up the texty parts.** Pull quotes, a lead line at larger size, labelled
  rows, numbered rows for measurement, tinted panels for the phase buckets. A
  section of four equal paragraphs is the failure mode.
- **Headings inside tinted panels are ink, bold, 16px, with a red underline.** Faint
  grey uppercase on a tint is invisible, which has happened.
- **What the client chooses, all through one component.** The working names, the
  Phase 1 guests, the Phase 2 guest profiles and the dream list are the same
  `choiceList`: cards with a tick and a cross, each list ending in an add-your-own
  box. `boxes` for names, `rows` for guests and profiles.
- **The tick and cross are tri-state, and unmarked is the default.** Nothing is
  pre-marked. Clicking the same button again clears it. A ticked card turns green
  with a green left edge, a crossed card fades. What that means for the export:
  unmarked exports as kept, a cross removes the item, and once anything in a list
  is ticked the export carries only the ticked ones from that list, which is how a
  client builds a shortlist rather than only pruning one. The sticky bar counts
  what is picked across all three lists, and says plainly that everything exports
  while nothing is.
- **Every list ends with an add-your-own box.** A dashed textarea on the sunk
  tint, with its own label and placeholder: "Add your own
  name", "Add your own names". Whatever the client types exports under that list,
  flagged as theirs. These are the highest-value controls on the page: a prospect
  who writes a theme or three names has started building the show with you, and
  guest names they type are introductions rather than cold outreach.
- **A guest card carries four fields.** Name in bold with a short region pill
  beside it (India, US, Europe, or To confirm), the role and company underneath in
  muted text, and below that the episode angle with a Source link where the title
  was verified against a public page. No verification dates against individual
  names. Phase 1 assumes the client's own introductions and says so.
- **The audience section is boxes, and the boxes are not selectable.** One card per
  group, the group name bold with a standalone sentence under it, never a bullet whose
  text runs on from the heading. No tick and cross: the client is not choosing an
  audience, and a selector on every section makes the page feel like a form.
- **Investment is ONE table (October 2026).** Columns: Item (with the opt-in tick and
  its tag), Rate, What it covers, Estimate. The Estimate cell shows the live amount
  and the working underneath ("₹60,000 × 12 episodes"). Each group (run by us, third
  party) ends in a subtotal row. The 6 and 12 month presets sit in a small panel
  above the table. The "Guests [Client] introduces" counter sits in its own row
  directly under Guest sourcing, because that is the line it changes, and the
  in-person episode counter sits under Offline recording, shown only when that line is
  ticked. There is no separate "Build your estimate" section: Akshay read the rate
  table and the estimator as the same information shown twice. Unticking fades the
  row, strikes the rate and drops the line from the export. Setup and post-production
  carry a locked tick. **The shorter preset is
  the default**, and the saved state must never override it on a first view: bump
  `STORE_VERSION` whenever the estimator or the guest list changes shape, so a
  stale configuration from an earlier build cannot decide what a prospect sees
  first.
  Setup and post-production are fixed rows, not toggles. Concessions are modelled as
  a count: three free recordings means the quantity starts at the episode count
  minus three.
- **State lives in localStorage**, wrapped in try/catch, so the prospect's
  configuration survives a refresh on their own machine and reaches nobody else.
  Render correctly when it comes back empty.
- **One export button in the sticky bar**, not a per-section download and not a CSV.
  It builds a Word file of the whole pitch carrying the guests they kept and the
  budget they configured.

## The HTML export

The page carries the `downloads` capability and builds the .docx client side with
the docx UMD bundle from cdn.jsdelivr.net, then hands it to
`claude.use("downloads")` and `save({filename, data})`.

Two things break it if forgotten. First, the tables: set `columnWidths` in twips at
the Table level, matching DXA widths on the cells and FIXED layout, exactly as the
build script does. Second, the export walks the DOM rather than holding a second
copy of the content, so a section removed from the page leaves the export
automatically, and a section added to the page needs no export change. Keep it that
way: two copies of the pitch text in one file will drift within a day.

## Checklist before presenting

**The October 2026 shape (Drizz review). Check these first:**

- The opener is four short paragraphs, under about 90 words, carrying one pitch: the
  podcast opens doors, and it builds the client's name because guests share their
  episode. The host is named by name in the first sentence.
- No cover strip under the title. No credibility line or preamble above the roadmap.
- No themes section anywhere. "Working names" is a main heading, three names, each
  with its tagline.
- "What an episode is" describes a broad conversation (career path, what worked,
  mistakes, their take on the subject, how they have changed this year), never one
  number.
- Only Phase 1 carries named people. Phase 2 is guest profiles at mid-market companies
  and startups. Marquee names sit in the Phase 3 dream list.
- "How we measure it" has no intro paragraph and no "titles are current" line.
- Investment is one table with an Estimate column and subtotals. No "Build your
  estimate" section. The introduces counter sits under Guest sourcing.
- Offline recording is described as post-production only. Crew, equipment and studio
  rental is its own third-party line at actuals.
- The studio setup consult applies to remote hosts too and says we introduce the
  consultant. No "no margin on hardware".
- Why us opens on the five-figure band, then the origin paragraph, then four cards of
  ⬩-separated companies, each labelled "Featuring founders of:" / "Featuring senior
  leaders of:" / "Featuring:".
- The file is a full HTML document with a doctype and the rate column wraps.

**Still true from earlier rounds:**

- Nowhere does the document describe a guest on an Unnati show as an Unnati client,
  or caption those names "trusted by".
- Every show name was run against `naming.md`, with a written verdict on all four
