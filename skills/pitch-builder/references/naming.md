# Naming the show

Read at Phase 5, before writing the working names into the themes section. This file
replaces the two-paragraph naming rule that used to live in `editorial-rules.md`,
which produced names Akshay rejected in three consecutive pitches.

Every rule here comes from a live calibration run in September 2026: roughly ninety
candidate names across three real prospects, each one marked works, maybe or no by
Akshay, with his reasons. The rejected names are in here too, because knowing what a
near-miss looks like is most of the skill.

---

## The calibration set

The fastest check on any candidate is which column it sits nearer. These are real
verdicts from a live session, not illustrations.

**Approved.** How India Lends. Credit Where Due. The Credit Economy. The Lending
Playbook. Banking Bharat. Lending at Scale. Credit in the Age of AI. The Future of
Credit. The Credit Playbook. The Lending Frontier. Human in the Loop. The Builder's
Manifesto. Augmented Humans. The Roadmap. Engineering Judgement. Engineering
Intelligence. Engineering in the Age of AI. The Future Engineer. The Human Stack. The
Engineering Edge. Systems of Record. The Engineering Manifesto. Architects of Trust.
Humans Who Ship. The Intelligent Stack. Insourced. The Future Classroom. Engineering
Outcomes. Augmenting Learning. Intelligent Learning Design. The Educator's Manifesto.
Teaching in the Age of AI. The Learning Curve. The Learning Economy. Future Faculty.
Teaching Machines. Beyond the Lecture. The Skills Manifesto. The Competence Curve.

**Rejected, and worth knowing why.** Who Wrote This (a question with one answer).
Nobody Learns by Watching (thesis). Let Them Break It (thesis, plus a collision).
Twelve Students One Machine (one episode's image). Cutover, Blast Radius, Rollback,
Seat Time, Par (trench jargon). The Thousand Crore Club (shrinks the client). The AI
Native Bank (locks a vertical). India's Credit Decade (locks a period). Cash Flow
First (true of any business). The Underwriters (grim, and confusable). The Borrowing
Economy (reads as a Bloomberg show). Practice Makes Perfect, What Students Keep,
Learning by Doing as a bare name (preachy). Who Builds It Now (reads as complaining).
The Next Curriculum (boring).

Note that "Insourced" is approved and "Cutover" is rejected, though both are single
technical terms. The difference is that insourcing is a direction a business is
moving, and a cutover is a bad night. Elevation is the whole distinction.

## What the name is for

The naming rule below is one instance of the rule that governs the whole skill:
**the show belongs to the guest's world, not the client's product.** The full version,
including why it matters and how to handle a client who pulls the show back toward
their category, is in `editorial-rules.md` under "What the show is about". Read that
first if the subject has not been settled yet, because a name cannot be better than
the subject under it.


The show is **surrogate marketing**. The client pays for it, but the show does not
belong to the client's product category. It belongs to the world the *guests* live
in. iXR Labs sells immersive labs, and their show is about the future of education.
Wissen does engineering for banks, retail and pharma, and their show is about senior
engineering judgement, not about banking.

So the name has one job: **a senior person who has never heard of the client, and
would never buy from them, should want to be on it and want to watch it.**

Everything below follows from that.

## The four tests

A candidate has to pass all four, and **every name carries a written verdict on every
test**. No blanks. A name with an unscored test does not ship.

Tests 1 and 2 are mechanical, so they get applied. Tests 3 and 4 are taste, and a run
that is short of good names will quietly skip them and ship on 1 and 2 alone. That is
exactly how "Cutover" reached a client as a lead recommendation. Score all four.

1. **Does it stay open?** A name the listener can exhaust has no repeat value. "Who
   Funds Bharat" keeps asking and keeps getting new answers. "Nobody Learns by
   Watching" is a thesis, and once you have heard it the name is spent.
2. **Does it belong to the guest's whole profession?** Not the client's vertical, not
   the client's product, and not a sub-segment. "The AI Native Bank" locks a company
   that also serves retail and pharma. "The Thousand Crore Club" tells a lender it is
   the wrong size.
3. **Is it elevated?** It should sound like the future of the field, not like a war
   story from inside it. The test is not whether the audience knows the word. They
   know all of them. The test is whether the word makes the field sound larger or
   smaller than it is. Cutover, Blast Radius, Rollback, Failover, Seat Time and Par
   all make it sound smaller.
4. **Would the listener be flattered?** Say both sentences out loud and write down how
   they land:
   - "You should listen to ___."
   - "We would like to feature you as ___" or "on ___."

   If the second sentence would embarrass a chief technology officer forwarding it to
   their communications team, the name is dead however well it scores on the rest.

### The self-exemption tell

**If you find yourself writing a sentence explaining why a rule does not apply to this
name, the rule applies.** The actual sentence from a failed run: "Cutover and Parallel
Run lean on migration vocabulary. That's the profession's shared language rather than
trench jargon." That is the run arguing with its own test, and it is the single most
reliable signal that a name should be killed. A name that passes needs one line saying
what it promises, not a defence.

## The shapes that work

Ranked by hit rate in the calibration.

**The broad-frame noun phrase.** The field, named at its largest honest scope.
*How India Lends. The Credit Economy. The Future Classroom. The Future Engineer.*

**[Field] in the Age of AI.** Borrows the energy of the moment and dates well enough,
because the age is not over. *Credit in the Age of AI. Engineering in the Age of AI.
Teaching in the Age of AI.*

**Reused jargon, elevated.** Take a term the profession already says and lift it out
of the trenches into an aspirational phrase. This is the highest-craft move and it
lands when the term carries a second, human meaning. *Agility by Design. Resilience by
Design. Continuous Delivery. Zero Downtime. Systems of Record. The Feedback Loop.
Risk Appetite. Compounding.*

**The idiom play.** A known phrase bent onto the field. *Credit Where Due. Learning by
Doing. Return on Trust. Proof of Learning.* Only when the bend is obvious in a second.

**Container words.** Manifesto, Playbook, Roadmap. They promise structure and repeat.
*The Builder's Manifesto. The Educator's Manifesto. The Lending Playbook. The
Roadmap.*

**The identity name.** The audience, named as the thing they would like to be.
*Augmented Humans. Humans Who Ship. Architects of Trust. Future Faculty. The
Underwriters* (rejected, but for its grim second meaning rather than its shape).

**The open question.** Works, despite reading like a headline. *Who Funds Bharat.
Build or Buy.* It works when the question has a different answer every episode, and
fails when it has one answer, which is why "Who Wrote This" was rejected and "Who
Builds It Now" read as complaining.

**The single repurposed word.** Rare, and excellent when it lands. *Insourced.
Immersed. Mastery. Creditworthy.* Must be a real word the field already uses, and
must sound good said aloud.

## The shapes that fail

Each of these was tried and rejected. They are listed with the actual names so the
pattern is recognisable rather than abstract.

- **The thesis statement.** Gives away the answer, so there is no reason to come back.
  *Nobody Learns by Watching. Let Them Break It.*
- **The episode image.** One Tuesday afternoon, stretched over fifty episodes.
  *Twelve Students, One Machine. The Empty Lab.*
- **Trench jargon.** Accurate, insider, and completely unappealing to a guest. This
  is the category a run most often talks itself into, because the words are real and
  the audience does know them. *Cutover. Parallel Run. Blast Radius. Rollback.
  Failover. Load Bearing. The Long Pole. Seat Time. Practicum* (a near miss: he liked
  the sound, not the meaning)*. Par. Delinquent.*
- **The failure state.** A name for the thing going wrong is a hard sell for a show
  the client puts their name on. *Rollback. Failover. The Rewrite.*
- **The size or segment lock.** Tells the client it cannot grow. *The Thousand Crore
  Club. Small Ticket. The AI Native Bank. The Immersive Classroom.*
- **The time lock.** *India's Credit Decade.*
- **True of any business.** *Cash Flow First. Build Well.*
- **Grim or confusable.** *The Underwriters* (reads as the insurance sense, and as a
  grim word)*. Delinquent.*
- **Preachy or obvious.** *Learning by Doing* (as a bare name, without a bend)*.
  Practice Makes Perfect. What Students Keep.*
- **Someone else's register.** *The Borrowing Economy* read as a Bloomberg show.
- **Sentences that are not catchphrases.** A full sentence works only when it is
  already an idiom. Otherwise it is a headline.

## How to generate

1. **Name the guest's field, not the client's product.** Write the field down in one
   phrase before writing any candidate. If the phrase contains the client's product
   category, start again.

2. **Generate twelve, spread by shape.** Not twelve variations of one idea. Two hard
   quotas, and they are the fix for the most common failure mode:
   - **At most three of the twelve from any one shape** in the list above.
   - **At most three sharing a root idea.** Twelve deployment words is one idea. So is
     Repeat Purchase, The Reorder and What Sells, which are all the buying-again idea
     wearing three hats. Write the root idea beside each candidate and count.

3. **Score all four tests, in a table, every name, no blanks.** Then kill on the
   scores. Expect to kill seven or eight.

4. **The floor: if fewer than five survive, generate another twelve.** Never ship the
   survivors of attrition. A short list is a signal that the first twelve were badly
   spread, not a licence to lower the bar. This is how weak names reach clients: the
   run kills its best candidates, finds three left, and ships those three with a
   written defence of each.

5. **Now check for collisions, and only now.** Collision-checking is expensive and it
   is not selection. Run it on the names that already passed on quality. A live
   podcast, a well-known book or film, or a self-help phrase occupying the name kills
   it, whatever its quality, and **a collision triggers a replacement rather than
   shrinking the set.** Go back to step 2 and generate for the gap.

   Two failure modes here, both seen in live runs. One: the check eats the whole
   effort, and the output becomes a report on collisions with three leftovers attached.
   Two: a collision is found and printed as a caution beside the name, which is what
   happened with "Let Them Break It" and Mel Robbins. A found collision kills the name.

6. **Compare the survivors against the calibration set** at the top of this file
   before presenting. If a candidate sits closer to the rejected column than the
   approved column, it is not ready, whatever the scores said.

7. **Present three, each making a different promise.** One line per name saying what
   it promises a listener, never what it says about the client. Pickable, with an
   add-your-own box, and none of them written as settled. **One line.** A name that
   needs a paragraph of justification is a name the run does not believe in.

8. **Read the last two pitches you built first.** Three names across three clients
   that all use the same shape is a template, and it is visible.

## Worked set

The three that shipped for Finezza, and why each survived:

- **How India Lends.** The biggest honest frame, and the best one on LinkedIn.
- **The Credit Playbook.** Promises method, which is what a guest thinks they are
  being asked for.
- **Credit in the Age of AI.** Puts the show where the sector is arguing right now.

None of them mentions lending software, which is what Finezza sells.
