# Founder Thesis Host Intro

The intro Akshay reads to camera or to mic at the top of the episode. It replaces the montage trailer, so it has to do the montage's job on its own: tell a listener who this person is, what the business is, and why the next hour is worth their time.

Two things make this skill different from every other intro format:

1. **It is spoken, not written.** It will be read aloud by a human. Every rule below flows from that.
2. **It is holistic, not a highlight reel.** It introduces the person and the business as a whole. It does not tease specific moments, decisions, dates, or reveals from the conversation. No "you'll hear why he pressed the red button in 2023." That belongs in a trailer, not here.

## Input

1. **The episode transcript.** Use it for the founder's own framings, the shape of the story, and the one insight that explains the business. Do NOT mine it for dramatic moments to tease.
2. **The research dossier** (usually a PDF in the project). Use it for the category, the origin story, credentials, and to fact-check.

Both are usually already in the project files. Don't ask for more.

## Structure

Six beats. The order of beats 1 and 2 can swap. Everything else holds.

### 1. Relatable hook

Put the listener inside their own life, using the product. This is the single most important line in the intro and the one most likely to come out generic.

> "Next time you order eggs on Blinkit or Zepto, there's a good chance you end up with a pack of Eggoz."

Pattern: **an everyday moment the listener has actually had + the brand showing up inside it.**

Good hooks name a real context: opening an app, standing at a kirana counter, a hospital bill, a salary slip, a wedding, a delivery at the door. Bad hooks describe a market ("India's protein consumption is rising") or address the listener abstractly ("we all eat eggs").

If the business is B2B and the listener has no direct contact with it, find the second-order contact: the product they use that runs on it, the bill they pay that it sits inside, the store they buy from that it supplies. If there is genuinely no relatable contact point, drop this beat and open on beat 2, then work the "and you've probably encountered it without knowing" idea in later.

### 2. Declarative identity line

> "Abhishek Negi is the founder of Eggoz, India's largest branded egg company."

Flat, confident, no hedging, no chained titles. "Founder of", not "co-founder and CEO of". One plain-English descriptor after the company name, in words a non-industry listener uses. "India's largest branded egg company", not "a D2C agritech platform in the packaged protein category".

If a superlative is used ("largest", "first", "biggest"), it must be defensible. See **Claim check** below.

### 3. The category gap, in one or two lines

Why this business gets to exist. Kept short and said the way you'd say it at a dinner table.

> "Eggs are a massive market in India and almost none of it is branded. There was no Amul of eggs."

The comparison line ("no Amul of eggs") is gold when you can find one. Reach for a company every Indian listener already knows and place the guest's business as the missing equivalent.

### 4. The founder's defining move

The one thing they did that no ordinary founder would have done. Origin, not episode moment.

> "So Abhishek, an IIT Kharagpur engineer whose first startup had just shut down, moved to a small town in Bihar and spent three years running a poultry farm."

This beat carries the credentials, so it does double duty. Fold the pedigree in as a clause rather than giving it its own sentence.

### 5. The core insight

The idea that makes the business make sense. Delivered as a short run of short sentences that build to a landing line.

> "Because he'd worked out something early. You can't tell a good egg from a bad one by looking at it. The shell hides everything. So the only way to control the egg is to control what the hen eats. That's the whole business."

This is the rhythmic peak of the intro. Three to five sentences, each shorter than a normal written sentence, the last one blunt. "That's the whole business." "That's it." "And that's the entire bet."

### 6. The fixed closer

Never varies in form:

> "In this episode of the Founder Thesis Podcast, [First name] shares the playbook for [outcome framed with difficulty]."

Use the full branded name, "the Founder Thesis Podcast". The outcome must name a difficulty bar: "building a brand in a commodity market", "building a profitable lending business in one of India's toughest sectors". "Shares the playbook" on its own is generic and fails.

**Target length: 110 to 150 words.** Roughly 45 to 60 seconds spoken.

## Why the length ceiling is hard

This intro occupies the most expensive minute on the channel.

Audience-retention curves for Founder Thesis episodes separate almost entirely between **second 30 and second 90**. At 30 seconds the best-retained and worst-retained episodes are indistinguishable — 72 to 75% of viewers still watching. By 90 seconds the best holds 55% and the worst holds 15%. After five minutes the curves run flat to the end: whatever happened in that minute has already decided the episode's watch time.

*(Probe of 3 episodes, lifetime curves, Sep 2026. Directionally strong, small sample — see the FT Channel Rules page for current status: https://app.notion.com/p/3d219a301a7f813fa919fe4b6c48a72f)*

Two things follow, and they are not negotiable:

1. **Never stack packaging.** This intro *replaces* the montage trailer. A trailer, then a cinematic title card, then this intro, spends the entire decisive window on packaging before the guest has said a word. Pick one. This is the one.
2. **Every beat must earn the next.** The existing "opening ten words" check applies to the whole piece, not just the open. If a sentence could be cut without the listener noticing, it is costing watch time, not building context. Go under 110 words rather than pad to reach it.

## Numbers rule

Akshay reads these aloud. Numbers are hard to say and harder to hear.

**At most two numbers in the whole intro. Zero is fine.**

Allowed:
- Approximations said the way people say them: "roughly a lakh crore rupees a year", "about a quarter of what the rest of the world eats", "three years", "a couple of days".
- Numbers that ARE the story. If the business is defined by the number, keep it.

Banned:
- Store counts, city counts, headcount, SKU counts, warehouse counts. Nobody cares that it's in 5,000 stores.
- Funding amounts and round names, unless the round itself is the story.
- Precise revenue or valuation figures.
- Percentages, grams, weights, thresholds, decimal places.
- Any number with more than two significant figures. "$36.88 million" is unsayable.

Test: if Akshay would have to slow down or take a run at the number, cut it or round it.

## Spoken language rules

Write the way a person talks, not the way a person writes.

**Do:**
- Contractions everywhere. "You can't", "here's", "there's", "he'd".
- Start sentences with And, But, So, Because, Now. This is how speech connects.
- Fragments are fine. "Building the shed, choosing the birds, designing the feed."
- Signposts people actually say: "Now here's the thing.", "And here's what's strange about it.", "What happened next is the interesting part."
- Vary sentence length hard. A long one, then a very short one. The short one is the punch.
- Say the plain word. "Poly bag", not "unhygienic packaging format". "Cheap to live there", not "lower cost of living".

**Don't:**
- No em-dashes or en-dashes. No semicolons. No parentheses.
- No written-only phrases. "Wafer thin", "leverage", "standardising a farm product across the country", "at scale", "unit economics", "value chain", "asset-light". If Akshay wouldn't say it in a bar, cut it.
- No jargon without unpacking: ARR, GMV, D2C, EBITDA, HoReCa, general trade. Say "kirana stores", not "general trade".
- No stacked adjectives or triple lists of abstractions.
- No clause-stacking. One idea per sentence.
- No direct quotes from the guest. Paraphrase into Akshay's mouth.
- No episode moments, no dates tied to a scene, no reveals.
- No banned intro words: masterclass, deep dive, journey, behind the scenes, fascinating, incredible insights, revolutionary, disrupting, game-changing, thought leader.

## Akshay's speaking voice

- A fellow founder, not a broadcaster. He recommends, he doesn't announce.
- Curious rather than impressed. He explains what he found interesting, and the listener borrows the interest.
- Plain Indian English. Indian reference points, Indian companies, Indian everyday life.
- Comfortable with a rhetorical setup: "Now here's the problem with an egg."
- Never sells. No "you don't want to miss this".

## Workflow

### Step 1: Draft 5 options

All five follow the six-beat structure. What varies is the entry angle and which idea carries beat 5.

Angle library:
1. **Relatable open** — everyday moment first, name second. The default.
2. **Declarative open** — name first, relatable line second.
3. **Person first** — open on the founder as a human being, then reveal the company. Works when the origin is unusual.
4. **Category gap first** — open on the missing brand in a huge market. Works when the "no Amul of X" line is strong.
5. **The core insight first** — open on the counterintuitive thing about the product, then the person who built around it.

Label each with its angle: `**Option 1 (Relatable open)**`.

### Step 2: Evaluate all 5 on spoken meter

Show this evaluation to Akshay. It's not an internal audit, it's part of the output. One short paragraph per option, honest about weaknesses.

Score each against these seven:

1. **Breath test.** Can every sentence be said comfortably in one breath? Flag any that can't.
2. **Landing lines.** Is there at least one short, blunt sentence that lands? Where is it?
3. **Stumble check.** Read it mentally. Number density, clause stacking, consonant pileups, tongue twisters. Name the exact phrase that snags.
4. **Voice check.** Would Akshay say it this way? Flag anything that reads written rather than spoken.
5. **Cold-listener check.** Does someone who has never heard of this company finish the intro knowing what it actually does? This is where "person first" angles usually fail.
6. **Opening ten words.** Do they earn the next thirty?
7. **Holistic check.** Is any beat leaning on a specific episode moment instead of introducing the business? If so, it fails the format, not just the meter.

### Step 3: Whittle to 3, edited

Pick the three strongest and rewrite them applying the fixes identified in step 2. Present them clean, as finished read-ready copy under short labels ("Relatable open, playbook close"). Say in one or two sentences which one has the best cadence and why.

The three should be genuinely different from each other in angle. Three variations of the same option is not a choice.

### Step 4: Claim check

End with a short flag, not a lecture. Two things to check:

- **Superlatives.** "India's largest" needs support in the dossier or the transcript. If it's only defensible in a narrower frame, say so and offer the narrower wording.
- **Numbers on tape versus audited numbers.** If the founder's spoken figure conflicts with the audited one, don't put either in the intro. Just note it.

One or two lines. Then stop.

## Worked example: Abhishek Negi, Eggoz

> "Next time you order eggs on Blinkit or Zepto, there's a good chance you end up with a pack of Eggoz. Abhishek Negi is the founder of Eggoz, India's largest branded egg company. Eggs are a massive market in India and almost none of it is branded. There was no Amul of eggs. So Abhishek, an IIT Kharagpur engineer whose first startup had just shut down, moved to a small town in Bihar and spent three years running a poultry farm. Because he'd worked out something early. You can't tell a good egg from a bad one by looking at it. The shell hides everything. So the only way to control the egg is to control what the hen eats. That's the whole business. In this episode of the Founder Thesis Podcast, Abhishek shares the playbook for building a brand in a commodity market."

~140 words.

**Why it works:**
- Beat 1 puts the listener in their own phone, holding a real app.
- Beat 2 is flat and declarative. No hedging.
- Beat 3 is two sentences, the second one four words long. "There was no Amul of eggs" does more than a paragraph of market sizing.
- Beat 4 folds IIT Kharagpur and the failed startup in as clauses, so the sentence is about the move to Bihar, not the CV.
- Beat 5 is the rhythmic peak. Five sentences, each short, ending on "That's the whole business."
- Zero numbers except "three years". No store counts, no funding, no revenue, no percentages.
- Nothing in it depends on the interview having happened. It introduces a person and a business.
