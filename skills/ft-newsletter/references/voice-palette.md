# Voice palette

Ranjita picks the theme; you pick the voice, with no stop. Default to **Akshay's plain voice** (below). Use a named voice only when the story clearly suits it, and say which and why in one line at the top of the output. In step-by-step mode (only when asked), offer 3 of these with a one-line "best when" each and wait for the pick.

Whatever the voice, every house rule still holds: everyday hook, plain words, one "For the curious" section, no em dashes, no brochure language, no negative guest framing, first-name references, CTA twice, comment invite at the end.

---

## Akshay's plain voice (default)
**Best when:** almost always. It is how Akshay writes his own newsletter.
- **Cold open:** an everyday moment the reader recognises, with "you" or a concrete person.
- **Engine:** honest, conversational first person. He admits what he didn't know, uses examples from his own life, and gives a clear opinion.
- **Pacing:** the story in steps, in the order things happened, the reader learning alongside him.
- **Close:** back to the opening image, his view, and a question inviting comments.
- **Watch for:** don't drift into report language. If a sentence sounds like a press release, rewrite it as he'd say it out loud. See `akshay-voice.md`.

## Michael Lewis
**Best when:** there's a vivid moment, a single character, or a number that quietly hides a whole story.
- **Cold open:** a small, almost mundane scene that contains the entire drama (a spreadsheet, a phone call, a decision made in a hallway). The reader feels the floor give way before you explain why.
- **Engine:** the outsider who notices what the insiders are politely ignoring. Tension comes from dramatic irony, we can see the thing coming.
- **Pacing:** scene, then zoom out to the stakes, then back to a person.
- **Close:** land on a human beat, not a summary. The reframe arrives through character.
- **Watch for:** don't over-narrate; trust the detail. One telling number beats five.

## Malcolm Gladwell
**Best when:** the insight reframes something the reader thought they understood.
- **Cold open:** an anecdote that seems unrelated, then becomes the key.
- **Engine:** "here's a pattern you didn't notice, and once you see it you can't unsee it." Counterintuition is the payload.
- **Pacing:** anecdote, principle, second anecdote that tests the principle, synthesis.
- **Close:** the pattern applied somewhere the reader didn't expect.
- **Watch for:** the reframe must be real, not a dressed-up truism.

## Ben Thompson (Stratechery)
**Best when:** the audience is sharp operators and investors who want the mechanical "why".
- **Cold open:** state the strategic question directly, no throat-clearing.
- **Engine:** frameworks and business-model logic. Value chains, moats, incentives, who captures the margin.
- **Pacing:** premise, framework, apply framework to the specifics, draw the implication.
- **Close:** the strategic consequence, stated as a clear judgment.
- **Watch for:** stay concrete; a framework with no specifics reads as abstract.

## Morgan Housel
**Best when:** the piece is about a decision, a behavior, or a timeless principle more than a news hook.
- **Cold open:** a short, calm observation about how people behave.
- **Engine:** psychology and long-run truths. Short paragraphs. Restraint.
- **Pacing:** principle, story that proves it, principle restated with more weight.
- **Close:** quiet, reflective, a line the reader keeps.
- **Watch for:** don't inflate; the power is in plainness.

## Paul Graham
**Best when:** the argument is contrarian and benefits from being stated cleanly, builder-to-builder.
- **Cold open:** a plain declarative claim that's slightly heretical.
- **Engine:** first-principles reasoning, essayistic, no ornament.
- **Pacing:** claim, reasoning, objection, answer to the objection.
- **Close:** the claim earned, now more general.
- **Watch for:** clarity over cleverness; cut anything performative.

If they ask for a voice not listed (e.g. Tim Urban's playful first-principles with vivid analogies, or a straight-ahead operator voice), take it. Capture the two things that matter: how that voice opens, and what it does at the close. Keep every house rule regardless of voice.
