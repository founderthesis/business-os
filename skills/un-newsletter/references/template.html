permissionless isn't symmetric

un#51

DeFi's protocol layer is open. Its execution layer is no longer the same game on each side of the trade.

There is a twelve-second gap between when an Ethereum transaction is broadcast and when it lands in a confirmed block. In that gap, the transaction sits in the public mempool, visible to anyone watching. For most of DeFi's first cycle, this transparency was treated as the system's central feature. Anyone could verify what was happening. Anyone could route through the same contracts. The mechanism worked the same way for everyone, which was assumed to mean it worked fairly for everyone.

By 2026, that assumption no longer survives contact with the data. The sophisticated half of the market stopped using the public mempool years ago. Institutional flow routes through private builder relationships, protected RPC endpoints, and encrypted intent-based aggregators that bypass the twelve-second window entirely. What's left in the public mempool is overwhelmingly retail. The same Uniswap pool now serves two different execution surfaces depending on which side of the trade you are on.

Permissionless was the architectural innovation, and it worked. It is now uncontroversial that anyone can access these protocols. What is less obvious, and what the empirical record from the last eighteen months is making undeniable, is that being able to access a protocol is not the same as being able to use it on the same terms as the participants on the other side of your trade.

the execution stack underneath

two completely different toolkits using the same contracts

To see the gap, look at the toolkit on each side.

the retail path: one mempool, two hops

A retail trader opens MetaMask, sets a slippage tolerance, and clicks swap. The transaction goes through the wallet's default RPC, into the public mempool, gets picked up by a builder, and lands in a block twelve seconds later. The path from signature to settlement is one or two hops.

the institutional toolkit: dozens of venues, private rails

An institutional execution stack looks nothing like this. Talos, which provides infrastructure for trading desks ranging from major banks down to small funds, connects to more than 100 venues1. GSR Markets maintains active connectivity to over 60 venues. Wintermute is plugged into more than 50 centralized and decentralized exchanges, with active inventory in nearly every high-cap token market. Amber Group processes approximately $5B in daily trading volume across more than 200 tokens, accounting for roughly 3% of global crypto trading activity2. Jump Trading runs a proprietary stack of more than 10,000 global compute nodes executing over 5 million trading simulations daily3.

This is not expensive infrastructure for the same activity retail does with more horsepower. It is a fundamentally different activity. An institutional smart order router does not trade against the slippage curve of a single Uniswap pool. It splits the order across dozens of venues, internalizes part of the trade against the firm's own book, hedges the residual on a centralized exchange before the on-chain leg confirms, and captures the cross-venue arbitrage spread. The retail trader and the institution can use the same Uniswap contract in the same block, and one is executing a swap while the other is running a multi-venue routing problem.

Underneath both sits the reference rate layer. Institutions do not price trades off CoinGecko. They license Volume-Weighted Median feeds and Time-Weighted Average Price calculations from providers like Coin Metrics, Kaiko, Amberdata, and CCData4, vendors whose pricing sits behind RFP processes and NDAs precisely because the data is a moat. The exact figures are guarded. Enterprise feeds at this tier are priced in seven figures annually. The bottom of the market literally cannot afford the inputs the top of the market treats as a baseline cost.

the migration in numbers: $27B that left the public window

The clearest single proof point of the migration is Flashbots Protect. By early 2024, it had served roughly 900,000 unique Ethereum addresses, protected more than 12 million transactions, and shielded over $27B in DEX trading volume from public mempool exposure5. That is twenty-seven billion dollars of flow that explicitly chose to leave the public window. Nobody who routes through Flashbots Protect, MEV Blocker, or BuilderNet is doing so because they want to lose a few basis points to a sandwich. They do so because, at some point, they understood the twelve-second window was no longer neutral infrastructure.

What is left in the public mempool is the flow that did not.

the misleading headline

why falling MEV revenue is evidence of a problem, not its solution

Average daily MEV revenue on Ethereum stabilised around $300,000 in 2024, down from over $500,000 daily in 2023, per Flashbots6. Read carelessly, this looks like progress. The extraction problem shrinking, the system maturing, the bots running out of opportunity. Read carefully, it says something else.

Headline MEV revenue measures what gets extracted from the public mempool. If sophisticated flow has migrated to private channels, the headline declines not because extraction has stopped but because the participants who were the most lucrative targets are no longer in the kill zone. What gets extracted at headline rates is what is left in the flow that lacked access to the alternatives.

Plot daily MEV revenue from 2021 to 2026 against the cumulative volume protected by Flashbots Protect, MEV Blocker, and BuilderNet, and the two lines move in opposite directions. The downward slope of the MEV revenue line and the upward slope of the protected-volume line are the same fact stated twice.

The attack surface that flow lands on is still large. A 2025 study in the Journal of Financial Economics by Hua, Tang and Yu found that 90% of Uniswap V2 blocks contained transactions vulnerable to front-running, with 16.1% containing confirmed sandwich attacks7. Independent empirical work has identified over 1.2 million sandwich attacks across Ethereum DEXs8. CoW Protocol, whose entire architecture exists because MEV is a problem, characterises MEV as a cumulative $1.4B problem for traders to date9.

The falling headline and the persistent attack surface are the same story from opposite ends. Institutional flow is built to avoid exposure, while retail flow goes through the public mempool by default.

the LP-side equivalent

how the same pool returns different yields to different participants

This dynamic is not limited to the trading side. The same asymmetry expresses itself, even more brutally, on the LP side.

LVR: stale prices, arbitraged systematically

The mechanism is Loss-Versus-Rebalancing. Constant function market makers like Uniswap V2 have no internal price discovery. When an asset's price moves on a venue with real price formation, like Binance, the AMM price stays stale until an arbitrageur trades against the pool to bring it in line. The arbitrageur systematically buys what is underpriced and sells what is overpriced, against the LPs. LVR is the quantitative cost of this, what a passive LP loses relative to what they would have earned dynamically rebalancing the same portfolio on a zero-fee reference exchange.

the empirical picture: a 40% gap on volatile pairs

Empirical work places LVR at 5–7% of total liquidity value annually across major AMMs10. On individual trades, it averages around 0.1% of pool value11. For volatile pairs like ETH/USDC, the cumulative effect is severe. Passive positions in volatile Uniswap V3 pools have been measured underperforming a simple buy-and-hold strategy by up to 40% over a sixty-day window, once impermanent loss and LVR are accounted for12. Sophisticated LPs running algorithmic range management, typically through systems like Gamma Strategies, reduce impermanent loss by 20–35% compared to static retail positions12.

The same pool, advertised at the same APY, returns radically different realised yields depending on whether the LP has institutional-grade tooling or not.

the quiet sorting: where retail liquidity ends up

The economics force a quiet sorting. Retail capital that wants to provide liquidity at scale ends up clustered in stablecoin pairs at the 0.01% fee tier, where LVR is negligible because the underlying assets do not move. These pools yield 3–8%, modest but survivable12. The high-yield volatile pools are mathematically hostile to passive capital. The only participants who can extract returns from them at scale are the ones whose execution stacks can rebalance ranges in milliseconds. The flow looks open. The returns are not.

what shows up in the data

where headline volume and committed capital diverge

The cleanest expression of the asymmetry is in the perp DEX market, where the gap between headline volume and committed capital has become impossible to ignore.

volume vs OI: where the divergence is the story

Monthly perp DEX trading volume first crossed the one trillion dollar mark in September 2025, peaked at $1.36T in October, and retraced to $699B by March 202614. The peak was driven heavily by incentive programs, airdrop farming, and a major liquidation cascade on October 10. None of these represented sustained institutional demand. The half-trillion-dollar deflation between October and March was the cycling-out of mercenary capital once incentives faded.

The structure that survived the deflation tells the story. Hyperliquid now holds between 66–73% of decentralised perp open interest, with around $9B in OI as of early May 2026 and weekly trading volumes above $50B15. Its lead survived a brief late-2025 disruption when incentive-driven competitors briefly captured short-term volume share before reverting.

The open-interest-to-volume ratio across the major perp DEXs makes the difference explicit. Hyperliquid sits at 0.64. Committed capital is roughly two-thirds of trading turnover. Aster sits at 0.18. Lighter at 0.1216. The volume on Aster and Lighter is real in the sense that trades happened, but the absence of meaningful committed capital tells you most of those trades were short-duration positions held by users farming incentives, not market participants warehousing risk overnight.

Open interest cannot be wash-traded without incurring real capital costs. Volume can. When the two metrics diverge by an order of magnitude, the divergence is the story.

what HIP-3 tells us: tokenized RWA, not crypto pairs

The HIP-3 finding sharpens this further. As of April–May 2026, seven of the top ten markets on Hyperliquid by trading volume are tokenized real-world assets, including silver, WTI crude, Brent crude, gold, S&P 500, Nasdaq 100, and individual equities, rather than crypto pairs17. Total open interest in HIP-3 tokenized RWA markets scaled from approximately $280M at the start of 2026 to $2.47B by mid-May18. The flow using these markets is structurally different from crypto-pair speculation. It is macro hedging, executed on-chain because the venues that traditionally serve macro hedging (CME, ICE, NYSE) are closed on weekends, holidays, and at night. The Hyperliquid order book is the only liquid place to trade tokenized oil when the Strait of Hormuz makes news on a Saturday.

The composition of where institutional flow actually lands is no longer abstract. It is observable on a public order book.

what design can still do

mechanism design is where DeFi has a real edge over TradFi

Mechanism design is the one area where DeFi has a real advantage over TradFi. Protocol behaviour is rewritable in ways that traditional market structure is not. Several mitigation patterns are now battle-tested.

The most successful is CoW Protocol's batch auction model. Orders are collected over thirty-second windows and cleared at a uniform price by competing solvers. The architecture removes the sequential ordering that sandwich attacks require. CoW processed $87B in 2025, more than double the $40.2B in 202419. Its volume growth has come at the direct expense of order flow that would otherwise pass through public AMM pools. The broader intent-based aggregator category (CoW Swap, UniswapX, 1inch Fusion) has effectively won the DEX aggregator wars by abstracting execution away from the public mempool entirely20.

The trade-offs are real and worth naming. Batched auctions take thirty seconds to settle, which makes them unsuitable for latency-sensitive trading. The shift to solver-based execution consolidates routing power in the hands of a small set of professional market makers, many of them the same firms that operate the institutional execution stacks described earlier. Private mempool adoption has scaled, but at the cost of letting large fractions of Ethereum transaction flow bypass the public gossip network. The system that emerges is more protective for retail users, more centralised at the execution layer, and a meaningful step away from the credibly neutral base layer DeFi started with.

Whether that trade is a feature or a bug depends on which problem you weight more.

in closing

what the next cycle has to defend

The first generation of DeFi defended a single principle, that anyone should be able to access the protocol. That principle won. It is now uncontroversial.

The second generation has to defend a harder one: that the people who do access the protocol should not be structurally disadvantaged inside it by virtue of using the tools they actually have available. The infrastructure for institutional execution on decentralised rails is already in place. The interesting question, the one that will define the next cycle, is what gets built on top of it, and for whom.
