# Built to Share (Satish Mugulavalli)

## Host

Satish Mugulavalli, founder of Hissa Fund (India's first SEBI-registered ESOP liquidity fund). Before Hissa: 4 years as Venture Partner at YourNest VC (analyzed 1,200+ startups, invested in 12), and 20+ years as a serial entrepreneur co-founding three networking companies across India and Silicon Valley.

The voice: someone who has seen the talent problem from every angle — founder who built teams, investor who watched teams break, now operator solving the ESOP liquidity gap. The intro should feel like a peer who actually knows the cost of a bad hire and the value of an early believer, not an HR consultant.

## Audience

Founders, early employees, and operators navigating India's startup talent wars. ~230k LinkedIn followers. They open episodes because they're either trying to hire against a unicorn that just raised, trying to retain a key engineer being poached, or wondering if their ESOP grants actually mean anything.

Assume they:
- Know the Indian startup ecosystem and the major players (Flipkart, Razorpay, Zerodha, Zepto, CRED, etc.).
- Understand basic compensation concepts (CTC, ESOPs, vesting, cliffs).
- Know the named talent battlegrounds (FAANG vs Indian startups, the great resignation, ESOP buybacks).
- Don't always know the mechanics of complex compensation structures (liquidation preferences, ratchets, secondary deals, RSU vs ESOP economics in detail).

## Target word count

**55-75 words.** Reads in about 25-35 seconds. Built to Share intros are shorter than Founder Thesis and stay tightly focused on the team-building lens.

## Currency preference

Mixed — both INR and USD work, **but pick one and stay consistent within an intro**. The Indian audience is comfortable with crores. Use USD when the figure is fundraise-scale ($140M raised), use INR/crore when the figure is more local-context (₹400 Cr in revenue, ESOP pool sized in crores). For company valuations, USD is cleaner.

## Structure (3 parts, in this order)

The defining feature of a Built to Share intro is that **the setup is purely about why this founder and company matter, and the talent/team angle only enters in the tease**. The setup establishes credibility and relevance. The tease then signals what specific people decisions get discussed in the episode.

Do not lean on team or talent numbers in the setup. No headcount, no attrition rates, no ESOP percentages, no hiring-war framing in parts 1 or 2. Those belong in part 3.

### 1. Guest + company line
Open with the guest and what they built. Use a standard business scale signal that establishes the company is real and noteworthy.

Pattern: `[Name] is the founder of [Company], [one-line company description with scale].`

Scale signals to use:
- Revenue or ARR ("a $50M ARR fintech")
- Capital raised ("raised $140M from Tiger Global and Sequoia")
- Users or customers served ("serving 30M Indian consumers")
- Market position ("the largest refurbished electronics platform in India")
- Notable outcome ("acquired by Reliance for $200M in 2024", "IPO-bound after raising $140M")
- Category-defining claim ("one of India's first SaaS companies to cross $100M ARR")

One primary scale signal, optionally paired with one secondary clause that adds context. Same rules as the other shows — don't stack three numbers.

### 2. Why the company matters
This is the part that earns the listener's 60 minutes. After the company line, surface **why this founder and company are worth listening to in the broader business sense** — not yet the team angle. The talent lens enters only in the tease.

Look for:
- Market position or category leadership ("the only profitable lending startup in a category that burned $500M in 2023")
- A pioneering move ("the first Indian SaaS company to crack the US enterprise market")
- Surviving or thriving through a hard moment ("one of the few D2C brands that survived the 2022 funding winter")
- Founder pedigree relevant to credibility ("ex-McKinsey, two prior exits, and now building his third venture")
- A notable strategic decision the market recognized ("pivoted from B2C to B2B in 2021 and tripled revenue the following year")
- A counterintuitive business model that worked ("profitable from year two in a sector where everyone else burns cash")

Pattern: **a specific claim about why this company or founder stands out**, in business terms. The reader should think "I want to know how they did that" — not yet about people, just about the result.

### 3. Episode tease — the "In this episode..." line
End with a tease that promises a **decision-level lesson about people**, not a generic "team-building advice" frame.

**Canonical podcast-name phrasing:** "Built to Share" (capital B, capital S, no "the", no "podcast" suffix — the name reads cleanly on its own). Use it in the tease line: "In this episode of Built to Share, Nakul breaks down..." The opening slot ("Nakul Kumar joins us on Built to Share...") also works but the tease slot is the default.

**Bad (generic):**
- "In this episode, Anurag shares how he built his team at CarDekho."
- "In this episode, Nakul talks about hiring and culture."

**Good (decision-specific):**
- "In this episode, Anurag explains how he convinced a senior PM to leave Google for a Series B startup, the ESOP math that closed the deal, and why he'd give away even more equity if he were starting over."
- "In this episode, Nakul breaks down the moment he decided to fire his entire founding tech team in year four, what it cost him, and the new hiring filter he built afterwards."

Pattern: **specific decision + concrete mechanic + reflection on what they'd do differently**. The third clause (the reflection or counterfactual) is what makes a Built to Share tease feel like reflection, not advice-giving.

## Hook library — 5 tease angles

1. **Specific people decision (default)** — a concrete hiring, firing, or compensation call with stakes. ("how he convinced a Google staff engineer to take a 50% pay cut, the ESOP math that closed the deal, and why it almost didn't work")
2. **The expensive lesson** — what they got wrong and would do differently. ("the cofounder split that cost him 18 months, what he missed in the original term sheet, and the legal clause every founder should add")
3. **Talent war story** — competing for scarce people against well-funded rivals. ("how he kept his ML team intact when CRED, Flipkart, and Google all came hunting in the same quarter")
4. **Compensation philosophy** — the mental model behind comp decisions. ("why he gave away 22% of the cap table in ESOPs before Series A, and the framework he uses to decide who gets what")
5. **Culture-defining moment** — the specific incident that shaped how the team works. ("the moment in 2021 when he realized his all-IIT engineering culture was breaking, and what he changed")
6. **The hire that changed the company** — one person whose joining shifted trajectory. ("how a chance LinkedIn message landed him a CTO who rebuilt the platform in six months")

Label each variation with its hook type (e.g., `Variation A (Specific people decision)`).

## What this podcast is NOT

The intro should never sound like:
- An HR or recruiting blog post
- A generic culture / values discussion
- A "leadership lessons" framing
- A success-story celebration

It should sound like: **a founder telling another founder about a specific people decision that cost them money, time, or sleep**.

## Recommendation guidance

Default to the **specific people decision hook** or the **expensive lesson hook**. The Built to Share audience is founders dealing with their own team problems right now. They want decisions and tradeoffs, not philosophy.

The expensive-lesson hook lands hardest when the guest is candid in the transcript about a real failure. The specific-people-decision hook lands when the transcript has a vivid story of a single hire, fire, or comp negotiation.

## Worked example

> "Nakul Kumar co-founded Cashify, India's largest refurbished electronics platform, which raised $140M and is now preparing for an IPO. He built the company from a single Delhi storefront to a 12-city operation that competes head-on with Flipkart and Amazon in a category most investors wrote off as unscalable. In this episode of Built to Share, Nakul breaks down the cofounder buyout that reset his cap table in year six, the ESOP refresh he wishes he'd designed earlier, and why he now thinks every founder over-pays in cash and under-pays in equity."

**Word count:** ~90 words. Slightly over target — could trim to 75 by tightening the middle clause.

### Annotation

- **Part 1 (guest + company):** Name, company, one-line description with business scale (largest in category, $140M raised, IPO-bound). No team numbers anywhere.
- **Part 2 (why the company matters):** A specific business claim — scaled from one storefront to 12 cities, competes with Flipkart and Amazon, in a category investors dismissed. The reader thinks "how did he pull that off?". Still no talent angle.
- **Part 3 (tease):** Now the talent / people / equity angle enters. Three specific things, all decision-level, with a reflection clause. This is where the show's actual subject matter lives.

The structure is: **business credibility first, talent insight only in the tease**. The listener should be sold on the founder before they learn what's specifically discussed.

## Style notes specific to Built to Share

- Satish's voice carries the weight of someone who has watched 1,200 startups try to solve this. Avoid making the intro sound like advice; make it sound like a peer comparing notes.
- ESOP / equity language is fine un-unpacked (vesting, cliff, cap table, refresh, buyback). The audience knows it.
- When citing comp or fundraise numbers, anchor them to a recognizable benchmark ("paying 20% below market", "raised $140M from Tiger", "competing with Razorpay's $100K offers").
- Don't mythologize the guest. Built to Share's voice is "we've all been there", not "this person is exceptional".

## Pre-interview mode

In pre-interview mode, only the dossier exists. Parts 1 (guest + company) and 2 (why the company matters) are unchanged — both are business-credibility content that comes from the dossier. Only part 3 (tease) shifts.

**Part 3 — tease shifts to anticipatory framing.** Replace transcript-based verbs ("breaks down", "explains how", "shares why") with anticipation verbs ("I want to understand", "we'll get into", "the questions I want to ask", "expect a conversation about").

The talent / team angle still enters only in part 3, same as post-recording. The difference is that the specific decisions are framed as questions Satish wants to explore, not answers the guest has already given.

Topic territory in Built to Share pre-interview mode draws from:
- The guest's company stage and scale (implied team challenges at that stage)
- Public knowledge about the company's hiring (named hires, ESOP buybacks announced, attrition press)
- The competitive talent landscape the company sits in
- The guest's prior ventures and what they likely learned about people

### Worked example (pre-interview version)

> "Nakul Kumar co-founded Cashify, India's largest refurbished electronics platform, which raised $140M and is now preparing for an IPO. He built the company from a single Delhi storefront to a 12-city operation that competes head-on with Flipkart and Amazon in a category most investors wrote off as unscalable. In this conversation on Built to Share, I want to understand how Nakul thought about equity at a company that took six years to find product-market fit, the people decisions he'd revisit if he were starting Cashify today, and where he sees the line between paying in cash and paying in ownership for the next generation of founders."

**Word count:** ~105 words.

### Notes

- Parts 1 and 2 are identical to the post-recording version.
- The three things in the tease are framed as questions ("how he thought about", "decisions he'd revisit", "where he sees the line") — not as transcript reveals.
- No talent numbers (headcount, attrition, ESOP percentages) appear in parts 1 or 2. Same rule as post-recording.

### Hook library adjustments

For pre-interview mode, the hook angles rephrase:

| Post-recording hook | Pre-interview equivalent |
|---|---|
| Specific people decision ("how he convinced a Google engineer to take a 50% cut") | Decision territory ("how he approached hiring senior engineers before the company had a logo") |
| Expensive lesson ("the cofounder split that cost him 18 months") | Reflection territory ("the people decisions he'd revisit if starting today") |
| Talent war story ("how he kept his ML team when CRED and Flipkart came hunting") | Competitive territory ("how he competes for engineers against three well-funded rivals") |
| Compensation philosophy ("why he gave 22% in ESOPs before Series A") | Philosophy territory ("how he thinks about the line between cash and equity") |
